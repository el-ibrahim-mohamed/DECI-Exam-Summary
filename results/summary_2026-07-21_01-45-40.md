## :blue[🌐 Digital World Fundamentals]

This topic defines what it means to live in a digital world, covering digital transformation, connected systems, digital assets, and data protection.

### :green[What is a Digital World?]

A **digital world** is a society where people, organizations, and services use computers, networks, and the internet for communication, work, learning, and accessing information.

### :green[Digital Transformation]

**:green[Definition:]** The process where businesses and people change how they do things, moving from old manual processes to using technology, computers, and the internet.
-   :yellow[**Example:**] Schools now use online learning platforms (e.g., Google Classroom) instead of only printed textbooks.
-   **:orange[Importance:]** Everything is becoming digital, making technology skills essential for all careers.

### :green[Connected Systems]

**:green[Definition:]** Devices and services that work together by exchanging information automatically over the internet.
-   :yellow[**Example:**] A smart home where your phone controls lights, temperature, and door locks, with devices communicating with each other.
-   **:red[Security Note:]** More connected devices increase security risks; use strong passwords and keep systems updated.

### :green[Digital Assets]

**:green[Definition:]** Things of value that exist only online and are important or worth money. You can't physically hold them.
-   **Personal Digital Assets:** Photos, videos, emails, social media accounts, games, digital money, documents.
-   **Business Digital Assets:** Company databases, customer information, software, websites, brand designs, employee records.
-   **:orange[Why Protect Them?]** Digital assets are valuable targets for hackers (e.g., identity theft from social media, financial loss from stolen company data).

### :green[Personal and Organizational Data]

**:green[Definition:]** Information about people and organizations (numbers, text, images, videos, location).
-   **Personal Data:** Identifying info (name, address, email, SSN), behavioral data (websites, location), health data, financial data.
-   **Organizational Data:** Customer info, employee records, financial reports, business secrets, product designs.
-   **:orange[Why Data Matters:]** Businesses use data to improve services; hackers steal data for identity theft, fraud, or blackmail.
-   **Basic Data Protection:** Use strong passwords, don't overshare online, be cautious of links, keep software updated.

### :violet[Concept Summary]

| Concept                  | What it is                                         | Example                                             |
| :----------------------- | :------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------- |
| **Digital Transformation** | Changing to use technology instead of old ways     | School using Google Classroom instead of textbooks  |
| **Connected Systems**    | Devices that work together through the internet    | Smart home with phone controlling lights            |
| **Digital Assets**       | Valuable things that exist only online             | Your social media account                           |
| **Personal Data**        | Information about you                              | Your phone number, photos, location                 |
| **Organizational Data**  | Business information                               | Customer database, employee records                 |

### ✅ Key Takeaways

-   The **digital world** is characterized by technology integration in daily life and business.
-   **Digital transformation** replaces manual processes with digital ones across all sectors.
-   **Connected systems** share information automatically, but require constant security vigilance.
-   **Digital assets** (personal and business) are valuable online items that need strong protection.
-   **Personal and organizational data** are critical to protect from misuse, fraud, and identity theft.

## :blue[🛡️ What is Cybersecurity]

This topic defines cybersecurity, outlines its three core goals (the CIA Triad), explains the importance of people, processes, and technology, and distinguishes it from information security.

### :green[Cybersecurity Definition]

**:green[Definition:]** The practice of protecting digital systems, networks, and data from theft, damage, or misuse by hackers and cybercriminals.
-   **:yellow[Example:]** Strong passwords and two-factor authentication protect your gaming account from hackers.

### :blue[🔐 Security Goals: The CIA Triad]

The three main goals of cybersecurity, often referred to as the **CIA Triad**, are:

1.  ### 🔒 Confidentiality
    **:green[Definition:]** Keeping information secret, ensuring only authorized people can access it.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] Your medical records are only visible to your doctor and authorized staff, not to the public.
    -   **Protection Methods:** Passwords, encryption, privacy settings, access control.

2.  ### 🎯 Integrity
    **:green[Definition:]** Ensuring information is accurate and has not been changed or corrupted without permission.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] A doctor prescribes 10mg of medicine; integrity ensures it remains 10mg in the system, preventing dangerous alterations.
    -   **Protection Methods:** Digital signatures, checksums, backups, version control.

3.  ### ⚙️ Availability
    **:green[Definition:]** Ensuring systems and information are accessible and functional when needed.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] Hospital systems must be available 24/7 so doctors can access patient records for urgent care.
    -   **Protection Methods:** System updates, backup systems, data backups, preventing attacks that cause downtime.

### :violet[People, Processes, Technology (PPT)]

Effective security requires balancing all three pillars:

1.  **Protecting People:**
    -   **Problem:** Humans are often the weakest link (weak passwords, clicking suspicious links, social engineering).
    -   **Protection:** Cybersecurity training, awareness of phishing/social engineering, fostering a security-conscious culture.

2.  **Protecting Processes:**
    -   **Definition:** Steps and procedures followed to keep systems secure.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] A password management process dictates creating strong, unique passwords and changing them regularly.
    -   **Protection:** Documenting clear procedures, training on procedures, regular checks and updates.

3.  **Protecting Technology:**
    -   **Definition:** Using technical tools and systems to defend against threats.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] Firewalls (control network traffic), antivirus software (scans for malware), encryption (scrambles data), two-factor authentication (extra verification).
    -   **:orange[Importance:]** All three (People, Processes, Technology) must work together; a weakness in one can compromise the others.

### :violet[Cybersecurity vs. Information Security]

| Concept                | Definition                                                     | Scope                                        |
| :--------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------- |
| **Information Security** | Protecting ALL information (digital and physical) from misuse. | Broader, includes physical documents.        |
| **Cybersecurity**      | Protecting specifically DIGITAL systems, networks, and data.   | Narrower, focuses on cyber threats.          |
-   **:orange[Key Difference:]** Information security covers all forms of information; cybersecurity focuses on digital threats.

### ✅ Key Takeaways

-   **Cybersecurity** protects digital assets from online threats.
-   The **CIA Triad** (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability) are the fundamental goals of all security systems.
-   Effective security relies on protecting **People, Processes, and Technology** simultaneously.
-   **Information Security** is a broader concept encompassing all data, while **Cybersecurity** is specific to digital data and systems.

## :blue[⚠️ Why Cybersecurity Matters]

Cybersecurity is crucial because its failure has severe impacts on finances, privacy, business operations, and national security.

### :red[Financial Impact]

Cyberattacks impose significant costs on both organizations and individuals.
-   **Direct Costs:** Paying ransomware, incident response, legal fees, regulatory fines (e.g., millions for data breaches).
-   **Indirect Costs:** Lost business/customers, severe damage to reputation, decreased stock prices, employee disruption.

### :red[Threat to Personal Privacy]

Cybersecurity failures expose personal data, leading to serious privacy risks.
-   **Data at Risk:** Full name, date of birth, SSN, location, photos, messages, financial data.
-   **:red[Consequence:]** **Identity theft** (criminals impersonate you, open credit, take loans), blackmail, reputational damage.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] Stolen SSN used to apply for credit cards, leaving you with debt and a ruined credit score.

### :red[Business Continuity]

**:green[Definition:]** The ability of an organization to continue its operations despite disruptions. Cybersecurity is vital for this.
-   **Disruption:** System downtime (e.g., hospitals can't access patient records, airlines can't operate), data loss (e.g., ransomware encrypts critical files).
-   **:yellow[Example:]** The **Colonial Pipeline ransomware attack (2021)** shut down a major fuel pipeline, causing gas shortages and a national emergency, demonstrating critical infrastructure vulnerability.

### :red[National Security]

Cyberattacks can impact entire countries and critical national defense systems.
-   **Critical Infrastructure at Risk:** Power grids, water systems, transportation, communication. Attacks can cause widespread blackouts, contamination, or transportation failures.
-   **Attacks by Other Countries:** Nation-state actors engage in espionage (stealing military/government secrets), sabotage (disabling power plants), and election interference.

### :red[Real-World Incidents]

-   **Yahoo Data Breaches (2013-2014):** Billions of user accounts compromised, destroying reputation and valuation. **Lesson:** The scale of impact from a single breach can be immense.
-   **WannaCry Ransomware (2017):** Spread globally, affecting hospitals, banks, and governments. **Lesson:** Unpatched software is a massive vulnerability, and critical services are highly susceptible.

### ✅ Key Takeaways

-   Cyberattacks incur huge **financial costs** (direct and indirect) for individuals and organizations.
-   Failure to protect data leads to severe **privacy risks** like identity theft and blackmail.
-   **Business continuity** depends on robust cybersecurity to prevent downtime and data loss.
-   Cybersecurity is a matter of **national security**, protecting critical infrastructure from foreign attacks.
-   Real incidents like WannaCry demonstrate the widespread and critical impacts of cyber failures.

## :blue[🔐 Core Security Principles: The CIA Triad]

The CIA Triad (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability) represents the foundational pillars of cybersecurity, guiding every security decision.

### 🔒 :green[Confidentiality]

**:green[Definition:]** Keeping information private and secret, accessible only by authorized individuals.
-   **Why it Matters:** Protects sensitive personal data (passwords, medical records) and vital business information (trade secrets, customer databases).
-   **How to Protect:**
    -   **Passwords:** Strong, unique, not shared.
    -   **Encryption:** Converts information into secret code, unreadable without a key.
    -   **Access Control:** Limits who can view information based on job role.
    -   **Privacy Settings:** Controls who sees your online data.
-   :red[**Failure Example:**] A hacker steals your medical records and sells them, leading to discrimination or public exposure.

### 🎯 :green[Integrity]

**:green[Definition:]** Ensuring information remains accurate, unaltered, and uncorrupted, reflecting exactly what was intended.
-   **Why it Matters:** Critical for accurate medical prescriptions, financial transactions, and academic records; alterations can cause severe harm or fraud.
-   **How to Protect:**
    -   **Digital Signatures:** Cryptographic proof that information came from a specific person and hasn't been changed.
    -   **Checksums:** Mathematical checks to detect even slight data alterations.
    -   **Backups:** Copies of data to restore from if corruption occurs.
    -   **Version Control:** Tracks changes to documents, allowing reverts.
    -   **Audit Trails:** Records who accessed/modified data.
-   :red[**Failure Example:**] A hacker changes a doctor's prescription from 50mg to 500mg in the system, causing patient harm or death.

### ⚙️ :green[Availability]

**:green[Definition:]** Ensuring systems and information are accessible and functioning reliably when needed.
-   **Why it Matters:** Crucial for critical services like hospitals, banking, and emergency systems; downtime can have life-threatening or financially devastating consequences.
-   **How to Protect:**
    -   **System Updates:** Regular fixes prevent crashes and errors.
    -   **Redundancy:** Having backup systems ready to take over if the main system fails.
    -   **Backups:** Regular data copies stored separately for disaster recovery.
    -   **Monitoring:** Watching systems for problems to fix them proactively.
    -   **Disaster Recovery Plan:** Procedures to restore operations after an incident.
-   :red[**Failure Example:**] A **DDoS attack** floods a website with fake traffic, making it unavailable to legitimate users, causing lost revenue and frustration.

### :violet[The Balance Between Principles]

Sometimes, protecting one CIA principle requires a trade-off with another.
-   :yellow[**Example:**] Strong encryption (Confidentiality) can slow down system performance (reducing Availability). Security decisions involve balancing these priorities.

### ✅ Key Takeaways

-   The **CIA Triad** (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability) are the three pillars of cybersecurity.
-   **Confidentiality** keeps secrets private, protected by encryption and access control.
-   **Integrity** ensures data accuracy and prevents unauthorized changes, protected by digital signatures and backups.
-   **Availability** guarantees systems are accessible when needed, protected by redundancy and monitoring.
-   A weakness in any one principle can compromise the entire security system.

## :blue[🧩 Additional Security Concepts]

Beyond the CIA Triad, four more principles (Authentication, Authorization, Accountability, Non-Repudiation) provide a deeper layer of security:
-   **Authentication:** "Are you really who you say you are?"
-   **Authorization:** "What are you allowed to do?"
-   **Accountability:** "What did you do?"
-   **Non-Repudiation:** "Can you deny you did it?"

### 🔑 :green[Authentication]

**:green[Definition:]** The process of proving your identity to a system.
-   **How it Works:** You provide credentials (e.g., username, password), and the system verifies if they are correct.
-   **Authentication Methods:**
    -   **Something You Know:** Passwords, PINs, security questions.
    -   **Something You Have:** Physical keys, ID cards, phone (for codes), security tokens.
    -   **Something You Are:** Biometrics (fingerprint, facial recognition).
-   **Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA):** Using two or more different types of authentication methods (e.g., password + phone code). This significantly enhances security.
-   **Strong Passwords:** Crucial for security; use long phrases (12+ characters) with a mix of uppercase, lowercase, numbers, and symbols. Avoid common words or personal info.

### 📜 :green[Authorization]

**:green[Definition:]** Determining what an authenticated user is *allowed* to do or access within a system.
-   **Relationship with Authentication:** Authentication *proves who you are*; authorization *determines what you can do*.
-   **Role-Based Authorization:** Access is granted based on a user's job role (e.g., a teacher sees all student grades; a student sees only their own).
-   **Principle of Least Privilege:** A core security rule to give users the absolute minimum access required to perform their job. This limits damage if an account is compromised.

### 📝 :green[Accountability]

**:green[Definition:]** Recording actions taken by users to track what they did and when.
-   **Why it Matters:** Creates an audit trail, allows investigation of incidents, proves who accessed or changed information, and enforces responsibility.
-   **Audit Logs:** Records all significant events: logins (username, time, IP, success/failure), data access (who, what file, read/modified), administrative actions (who changed permissions).

### 🚫 :green[Non-Repudiation]

**:green[Definition:]** The inability to deny that an action was performed because there is undeniable proof.
-   **How it Works:** Primarily achieved through **digital signatures**, which are like unique electronic fingerprints for documents or transactions. If data is changed, the signature becomes invalid.
-   **Why it Matters:** Provides legal and business protection by proving agreement or authorization, preventing disputes and fraud.

### ✅ Key Takeaways

-   **Authentication** verifies identity, often strengthened by **MFA** and **strong passwords**.
-   **Authorization** defines access rights based on identity, following the **Principle of Least Privilege**.
-   **Accountability** tracks and records actions, enabling investigations through **audit logs**.
-   **Non-Repudiation** provides undeniable proof of actions, often using **digital signatures**.
-   These four principles, along with the CIA Triad, form a comprehensive security framework.

## :blue[🎯 Assets and Attack Surfaces]

This topic covers what constitutes an asset in cybersecurity, different asset types, the concept of an attack surface, and the importance of managing your digital footprint.

### :green[Assets]

**:green[Definition:]** Anything of value that needs protection from cyberattacks and security threats, whether physical or digital.
-   :yellow[**Examples:**] Your social media account (digital), your laptop (physical), a company's customer database (digital).

### :violet[Types of Assets]

1.  **Information Assets:** Data and information with value.
    -   :yellow[**Examples:**] Personal: SSN, medical records, photos. Business: customer lists, trade secrets, financial data.
2.  **Technology Assets:** Computers, devices, and systems that process information.
    -   :yellow[**Examples:**] Devices (phones, servers), software (operating systems, applications), infrastructure (networks, cloud storage).
3.  **People Assets:** Human resources, their knowledge, and skills.
    -   :yellow[**Examples:**] Employees, users. They are valuable targets for social engineering.
4.  **Reputation Assets:** Trust and brand image.
    -   :yellow[**Examples:**] Company brand, customer trust, personal professional image. Can be damaged by breaches or false information.
5.  **Physical Assets:** Tangible things you can touch.
    -   :yellow[**Examples:**] Office buildings, servers, laptops, USB drives.

-   **Asset Valuation:** Organizations rank assets by importance (Critical, High, Medium, Low) to prioritize protection.

### :red[Attack Surface]

**:green[Definition:]** All the points where an unauthorized user (hacker) could try to break into a system or network. It's the sum of all vulnerabilities and entry points.
-   :yellow[**Analogy:**] All the doors, windows, and weak spots of a house that a burglar could use.
-   **Components:** Network entry points (routers, servers), software vulnerabilities (outdated software, apps), user behavior (weak passwords, phishing), physical entry points (unlocked doors, public Wi-Fi).
-   **Small vs. Large:** A smaller attack surface means fewer entry points, making it harder for attackers.
-   **How to Reduce Attack Surface:**
    -   **Technology Changes:** Keep software updated, remove unnecessary software/services, use firewalls.
    -   **User Behavior:** Use strong passwords, avoid clicking suspicious links, keep credentials safe.
    -   **Organizational Changes:** Apply Principle of Least Privilege, use network segmentation, conduct security audits.

### :red[Digital Footprint]

**:green[Definition:]** All the information about you that exists online, left behind as you use the internet.
-   :yellow[**Analogy:**] Footprints you leave in the snow.
-   **Components:** Emails, online accounts, location data, online purchases, social media posts, public records.
-   **Active Footprint:** Information you intentionally share (posts, accounts).
-   **Passive Footprint:** Information collected about you without your direct action (browsing history, cookies, data brokers).
-   **:red[Why it Matters:]** Creates security risks (hackers use info for targeting/scams), privacy risks (companies track/sell data), and reputation risks (old posts can resurface).
-   **How to Manage Digital Footprint:**
    -   Reduce what you share, use privacy settings, be aware of data collection, regularly clean up old posts/accounts.

### ✅ Key Takeaways

-   An **asset** is anything of value needing protection (information, technology, people, reputation, physical).
-   The **attack surface** is the sum of all potential entry points for hackers, which should be minimized.
-   Your **digital footprint** is your online data trail, posing security, privacy, and reputation risks.
-   Proactive management of both attack surface and digital footprint is crucial for cybersecurity.

## :blue[💎 Understanding Assets (Detailed)]

This topic delves deeper into distinct asset categories, particularly for technology, and explains why understanding them is key to studying threats.

### :green[Assets Refresher]

Assets are anything of value to an organization that needs protection. Attackers target assets because they are valuable or can cause damage if compromised.

### :violet[Key Asset Types and Their Vulnerabilities]

1.  ### :green[Data Assets]
    **:green[Definition:]** Information in various forms (numbers, text, images, videos, files). Often the most valuable asset to attackers.
    -   :yellow[**Examples:**] Patient medical records, customer databases, trade secrets, financial information.
    -   **:red[How Attacked:]** Theft, corruption, deletion, exposure.

2.  ### :green[System Assets]
    **:green[Definition:]** Computers and servers that process and store data (the machines that run everything).
    -   :yellow[**Examples:**] Laptops, desktop computers, web servers, database servers, smartphones, IoT devices.
    -   **:red[How Attacked:]** Malware infection, exploitation of vulnerabilities, Denial of Service (DoS), physical damage/theft.

3.  ### :green[Application Assets]
    **:green[Definition:]** Software programs that run on systems and perform specific tasks.
    -   :yellow[**Examples:]** Word processors, CRM systems, banking apps, social media apps, custom internal software.
    -   **:red[How Attacked:]** Code vulnerabilities, SQL injection, Cross-Site Scripting (XSS), weak authentication.

4.  ### :green[Infrastructure Assets]
    **:green[Definition:]** The physical and network foundation upon which everything else depends.
    -   :yellow[**Examples:**] Network routers, switches, firewalls, data centers, server rooms, cloud servers, power grids (critical infrastructure).
    -   **:red[How Attacked:]** DDoS attacks, network interception, physical attacks (e.g., cutting cables), configuration issues.

5.  ### :green[Human Assets]
    **:green[Definition:]** People, their knowledge, and skills. They are considered assets because they use systems, make decisions, and can be manipulated.
    -   :yellow[**Examples:]** Employees, contractors, customers, students.
    -   **:red[How Compromised:]** Social engineering, phishing, malware spread through user actions, blackmail.

### :green[Asset Inventory and Valuation]

Organizations maintain an **asset inventory** (a list of all assets) and perform **asset valuation** (assessing replacement cost, downtime cost, data value, regulatory importance) to prioritize protection.
-   **:yellow[Example:]** Hospital patient medical record systems are **critical assets** due to life safety implications, requiring the highest protection.

### ✅ Key Takeaways

-   Assets are diverse, ranging from **data** and **systems** to **applications**, **infrastructure**, and **human assets**.
-   Each asset type has specific vulnerabilities and attack methods.
-   **Data** is often the primary target of attackers.
-   **Human assets** are critical and often the weakest link due to susceptibility to manipulation.
-   Organizations must identify, categorize, and value their assets to implement effective defenses.

## :blue[⚠️ Threats, Vulnerabilities, Risks, and Attacks]

These four terms are foundational to cybersecurity, describing the stages and elements of potential harm. Understanding their distinct meanings and relationships is crucial.

### :green[Threat]

**:green[Definition:]** Anything that could potentially harm an asset, system, or organization. It's the source of potential danger.
-   **:orange[Key Idea:]** A threat exists whether or not you have defenses against it. It's about intent and capability.
-   :yellow[**Examples:**] Cybercriminals (stealing data), natural disasters (earthquakes), careless employees (accidents), nation-state actors (espionage).
-   **Types:** Internal (disgruntled employees, accidental mistakes) or External (hackers, foreign governments).

### :red[Vulnerability]

**:green[Definition:]** A weakness or flaw in a system, application, or process that can be exploited by a threat.
-   **:orange[Key Idea:]** A vulnerability exists even if no one exploits it. It's a weakness waiting to be discovered.
-   :yellow[**Examples:**] Unpatched software, weak passwords, misconfigured firewalls, untrained employees, unlocked doors, missing backup procedures.

### :red[Risk]

**:green[Definition:]** The probability that a threat will exploit a vulnerability and cause harm, combined with the impact (damage) it would cause.
-   **:orange[Key Idea:]** Risk depends on three things: the existence of a **threat**, a **vulnerability** for it to exploit, and the potential **impact** of a successful exploitation.
-   **Risk Formula:** `Risk = Threat x Vulnerability x Impact`
-   **Risk Assessment:** Organizations evaluate risk to prioritize protection (High, Medium, Low). The goal is **risk reduction**, not elimination, as perfect safety is impossible.

### :red[Attack]

**:green[Definition:]** When a threat actively takes action to exploit a vulnerability. It's the actual attempt to cause harm.
-   **:orange[Key Idea:]** An attack is an active, deliberate exploitation of a weakness.
-   :yellow[**Examples:**] A malware infection, a phishing attempt, a DDoS attack, a data breach.

### :violet[How They Connect]

-   A **Threat** (e.g., a cybercriminal) seeks to exploit a **Vulnerability** (e.g., unpatched software).
-   The combination of a Threat and a Vulnerability creates a **Risk** (e.g., high likelihood of a system being breached with severe consequences).
-   An **Attack** is the actual event where the Threat exploits the Vulnerability.
-   **:orange[Prevention:]** The most effective way to manage risk is to **eliminate vulnerabilities**, as threats often cannot be eliminated.

### :violet[Real-World Connection]

-   **:yellow[WannaCry Ransomware (2017):]**
    -   **Threat:** Cybercriminals wanting money.
    -   **Vulnerability:** Many computers had unpatched software.
    -   **Attack:** Ransomware spread and locked files.
    -   **Result:** Billions in losses, disrupted hospitals.
    -   **:orange[Lesson:]** Keeping software updated prevents most damage.

### ✅ Key Takeaways

-   A **threat** is a potential danger, and a **vulnerability** is a weakness.
-   **Risk** is the probability of a threat exploiting a vulnerability and the resulting impact.
-   An **attack** is the actual exploitation of a vulnerability by a threat.
-   These four concepts are interconnected, and eliminating vulnerabilities is key to reducing risk.

## :blue[🎭 Threat Actors]

Threat actors are the individuals or groups behind cyber threats. Identifying them is vital because their motivations and skill levels dictate the types of attacks they'll launch and how to defend against them.

### :violet[Types of Threat Actors]

1.  ### :red[Cybercriminals]
    -   **Motivation:** Primarily **financial gain** (stealing money, selling data, ransomware, extortion).
    -   **Characteristics:** Most widespread, ranging from individuals to organized groups.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] A group sending fraudulent emails to steal payment card details.

2.  ### :red[Nation-State Actors]
    -   **Motivation:** **Political or military aims** (espionage, sabotage of critical infrastructure, intelligence gathering, election interference).
    -   **Characteristics:** Most dangerous and capable, backed by enormous government resources and highly skilled experts.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] A government-sponsored group targeting another country's power grid.

3.  ### :red[Hacktivists]
    -   **Motivation:** To support a **cause or message** (protest, expose perceived wrongs). Not financial.
    -   **Characteristics:** Use intrusion as a form of protest (e.g., defacing websites, leaking information).
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] Changing a company's website to display a political message.

4.  ### :red[Insider Threats]
    -   **Motivation:** Varies; can be **deliberate** (disgruntled employee, greed) or **accidental** (carelessness, human error).
    -   **Characteristics:** Originates from within the organization (employees, contractors) who already have legitimate access. Especially dangerous due to existing permissions.
    -   :yellow[**Example (Accidental):**] An employee unknowingly clicks a fraudulent email link, compromising the company network.

5.  ### :red[Script Kiddies]
    -   **Motivation:** Curiosity, proving themselves, or seeking recognition.
    -   **Characteristics:** Inexperienced attackers using ready-made tools created by others, often without full understanding. Least skilled but most numerous.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] A teenager using a free tool downloaded online to try and disrupt a small website.

### :violet[Threat Actor Summary]

| Threat Actor          | Main Motivation                 | Key Feature                                     |
| :-------------------- | :------------------------------ | :---------------------------------------------- |
| **Cybercriminals**    | Money                           | Most widespread type                            |
| **Nation-State Actors** | Politics or military aims       | Most dangerous and capable                      |
| **Hacktivists**       | A cause or a message            | Use intrusion as protest                        |
| **Insider Threats**   | Varies; deliberate or accidental | Danger from inside the organization             |
| **Script Kiddies**    | Curiosity or proving themselves | Inexperienced, use ready-made tools             |

### ✅ Key Takeaways

-   **Threat actors** are the individuals or groups behind cyber threats.
-   Their **motivation and skill level** vary significantly, influencing their tactics.
-   **Cybercriminals** seek financial gain; **Nation-state actors** pursue political/military objectives.
-   **Hacktivists** protest for a cause; **Insider threats** originate from within (deliberate or accidental).
-   **Script kiddies** are inexperienced but can still cause harm.

## :blue[🔄 Attack Lifecycle]

An attack is not a single event but a progression through several stages. Understanding this **attack lifecycle** allows defenders to detect and stop attacks early, minimizing damage.

### :violet[The Six Stages of an Attack]

1.  ### 🔎 :green[Reconnaissance]
    -   **Attacker Action:** Gathers information about the target *before* the attack.
    -   :yellow[**Analogy:**] A burglar watching a building to learn routines and entry points.
    -   **:orange[Connection:]** Exploits publicly available information (digital footprint).

2.  ### 🚪 :green[Initial Access]
    -   **Attacker Action:** Gains the first entry into the system.
    -   :yellow[**Analogy:**] A burglar finding an open door or window and entering.
    -   **:orange[Importance:]** First chance to detect the intruder inside. Often achieved via phishing or weak passwords.

3.  ### 🏃 :green[Execution]
    -   **Attacker Action:** Runs malicious programs (malware) or carries out actions within the system.
    -   :yellow[**Analogy:**] A burglar actually searching the building for valuables.

4.  ### 🗝️ :green[Persistence]
    -   **Attacker Action:** Creates a way to return to the system, even if the initial entry point is closed (e.g., installing a backdoor).
    -   :yellow[**Analogy:**] A burglar making a copy of a key to re-enter later.
    -   **:orange[Defender Note:]** When removing an attacker, confirm no backdoors are left.

5.  ### ⏩ :green[Lateral Movement]
    -   **Attacker Action:** Moves from the initially compromised device to other devices and systems within the same network.
    -   :yellow[**Analogy:**] A burglar moving from room to room in search of more valuable items.
    -   **:orange[Defender Note:]** Network monitoring helps detect unusual movement.

6.  ### 📤 :green[Exfiltration]
    -   **Attacker Action:** Transfers stolen data out of the target network to their own control.
    -   :yellow[**Analogy:**] A burglar carrying stolen valuables out of the building.
    -   **:red[Impact:]** This is where the actual harm and data loss are realized.

### :violet[Attack Lifecycle Summary]

| Stage             | What the Attacker Does                  | Burglar Example                  |
| :---------------- | :-------------------------------------- | :------------------------------- |
| **Reconnaissance** | Gathers information about the target    | Watching the building            |
| **Initial Access** | Gains the first entry                   | Entering through an open door    |
| **Execution**     | Runs malicious actions                  | Searching for valuables          |
| **Persistence**   | Secures a way to return                 | Copying a key                    |
| **Lateral Movement** | Moves to other systems                  | Going room to room               |
| **Exfiltration**  | Takes the data out                      | Carrying the goods away          |

### ✅ Key Takeaways

-   The **attack lifecycle** describes the sequential stages of a cyberattack.
-   Understanding these stages allows defenders to intercept attacks at any point.
-   Early detection (e.g., at **Initial Access**) significantly reduces potential damage.
-   The stages flow from **Reconnaissance** to **Exfiltration**, each building on the previous one.
-   **Persistence** and **Lateral Movement** are crucial for attackers to deepen their foothold.

## :blue[🎣 Social Engineering Attacks]

**Social engineering** is a type of attack that manipulates human emotions and trust to deceive individuals into revealing information or granting access, rather than exploiting technical vulnerabilities. It targets people, not machines.

### :violet[Forms of Social Engineering]

1.  ### :red[Phishing]
    -   **Method:** Fraudulent message (usually email) pretending to be from a trusted source (bank, popular company).
    -   **Purpose:** Deceive recipients into clicking a harmful link or entering credentials on a fake website.
    -   **Warning Signs:** Urgency/fear, spelling mistakes, unfamiliar sender, incorrect link addresses.
    -   **Protection:** Never click links in unverified messages; always go directly to the official website.

2.  ### :red[Spear Phishing]
    -   **Method:** Highly targeted phishing attack aimed at a *specific individual*.
    -   **Key Feature:** Attackers gather personal information (name, interests, job role) to craft a highly convincing and personalized message. More dangerous than generic phishing.

3.  ### :red[Vishing]
    -   **Method:** Deception conducted via a **telephone call** (voice + phishing).
    -   **Purpose:** Attacker pretends to be from a trusted organization (bank, tech support) to persuade the victim to reveal sensitive information over the phone.
    -   **Protection:** A genuine organization will **never** ask for your password or secret code over the phone. End the call and contact the official organization directly using a verified number.

4.  ### :red[Smishing]
    -   **Method:** Deception conducted via **SMS** (text message + phishing).
    -   **Purpose:** Sends text messages with harmful links (e.g., fake prize claims) or requests for information.
    -   **Protection:** Be wary of messages promising unusual rewards or creating urgency; do not click suspicious links.

5.  ### :red[Pretexting]
    -   **Method:** Attacker invents a complete, false story or identity to win the victim's trust and extract information.
    -   **Key Feature:** Involves building a convincing scenario, not just a single message (e.g., pretending to be IT support asking for a password to fix an account problem).
    -   **Protection:** Always verify a person's identity through official channels before providing any information, no matter how convincing the story.

### :violet[Social Engineering Summary]

| Form             | Method                           | Key Feature                              |
| :--------------- | :------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------- |
| **Phishing**     | Fraudulent message to many people | Broad and general, seeks credentials     |
| **Spear Phishing** | Message aimed at one person      | Tailored and highly convincing           |
| **Vishing**      | Deception by phone call          | Uses voice to extract info               |
| **Smishing**     | Deception by text message        | Uses SMS links for traps                 |
| **Pretexting**   | A complete invented story        | Builds false trust for information gain  |

### ✅ Key Takeaways

-   **Social engineering** manipulates people's trust and emotions, making them a target.
-   **Phishing** (broad email) and **spear phishing** (targeted email) use fake messages to steal credentials.
-   **Vishing** (phone calls) and **smishing** (text messages) use voice or SMS for deception.
-   **Pretexting** involves an elaborate, fabricated story to gain trust.
-   The strongest protection is **skepticism** and **verification** before acting or sharing information.

## :blue[🦠 Malware Attacks]

**Malware** (malicious software) is any harmful program designed to damage a device, disrupt operations, or steal data. Understanding different types helps in recognizing and defending against them.

### :violet[Forms of Malware]

1.  ### :red[Virus]
    -   **Behavior:** Attaches itself to a legitimate file or program and spreads when that file is run/opened by a user.
    -   **Key Feature:** Requires user action (e.g., opening an infected document) and a host file to spread.
    -   :yellow[**Analogy:**] A common cold that spreads through contact.

2.  ### :red[Worm]
    -   **Behavior:** Spreads by itself across networks without needing to attach to a file or require user action.
    -   **Key Feature:** Self-replicating and can infect many devices rapidly (e.g., exploiting a software vulnerability). More dangerous in spread than a virus.
    -   :yellow[**Analogy:**] An illness spreading through the air.

3.  ### :red[Trojan]
    -   **Behavior:** Disguises itself as a legitimate and useful program, tricking users into installing it.
    -   **Key Feature:** Appears attractive on the outside but conceals malicious code within, often opening a "backdoor" for attackers.
    -   **Protection:** Only download programs from official, trusted sources.

4.  ### :red[Spyware]
    -   **Behavior:** Secretly observes user activity and collects information (keystrokes, screen images, browsing history) without their knowledge, sending it to the attacker.
    -   **Key Feature:** Operates silently in the background, making detection difficult.
    -   **Signs:** Device suddenly slows down, unusual internet activity.
    -   **Protection:** Use antivirus software, avoid suspicious programs.

5.  ### :red[Ransomware]
    -   **Behavior:** Locks a user's files or entire device by encrypting them, then displays a message demanding payment (ransom) to unlock them.
    -   **Key Feature:** One of the most dangerous forms, capable of halting entire organizations (e.g., hospitals, businesses).
    -   **Protection:** The most important defense is regularly **backing up files to a separate, offline location**.

### :violet[Malware Summary]

| Malware Type  | Behavior                                     | Key Feature                                     |
| :------------ | :------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------- |
| **Virus**     | Attaches to a file, spreads when opened      | Needs a user to act, requires a host file       |
| **Worm**      | Spreads by itself across a network           | Needs no user action, self-replicating          |
| **Trojan**    | Disguises as a safe, useful program          | Hidden danger, often creates backdoors          |
| **Spyware**   | Secretly watches and collects information    | Operates in silence, hard to detect             |
| **Ransomware** | Locks files/device and demands payment      | Demands a ransom for data/system access         |

### ✅ Key Takeaways

-   **Malware** is any malicious software designed to harm or steal data.
-   **Viruses** need a host file and user action; **worms** self-propagate across networks.
-   **Trojans** deceive users by appearing legitimate; **spyware** silently monitors activities.
-   **Ransomware** locks data and demands payment, making backups crucial.
-   Common protections include reputable sources, antivirus, regular updates, and consistent backups.

## :blue[🌐 Network-Based Attacks]

Network-based attacks target the connections between devices, disrupting services, intercepting data, or redirecting users. Each attack typically threatens specific principles of the CIA Triad.

### :violet[Types of Network Attacks]

1.  ### :red[Denial-of-Service (DoS) / Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDoS)]
    -   **What it Does:** Floods a website or server with an overwhelming volume of false requests, causing the service to become unavailable or crash. DDoS uses multiple compromised systems for the attack.
    -   **CIA Principle Threatened:** **Availability** (prevents legitimate users from accessing the service).
    -   :yellow[**Analogy:**] A small shop crowded by thousands of fake customers, blocking real ones.

2.  ### :red[Man-in-the-Middle (MITM) Attack]
    -   **What it Does:** An attacker secretly positions themselves between two communicating parties (e.g., user and website), intercepting, reading, and potentially altering all data passing between them.
    -   **CIA Principles Threatened:** **Confidentiality** (attacker reads messages) and **Integrity** (attacker can change messages).
    -   **Protection:** Use **HTTPS** (the 'S' indicates an encrypted connection), avoid sensitive activities on public Wi-Fi.

3.  ### :red[DNS Attack]
    -   **What it Does:** Tampering with the **Domain Name System (DNS)**, which acts like the internet's phonebook, translating website names (like google.com) into IP addresses. An attack redirects users typing a genuine website name to a fake, malicious site.
    -   **CIA Principles Threatened:** **Confidentiality** (info entered on fake site is stolen) and **Integrity** (website address is altered).
    -   **Protection:** Always verify the website address (URL) and look for the **HTTPS** indicator before entering sensitive information.

4.  ### :red[Packet Sniffing]
    -   **What it Does:** Capturing and reading small pieces of data (**packets**) as they travel across a network. If data is unencrypted, the attacker can see everything sent and received, including passwords.
    -   **CIA Principle Threatened:** **Confidentiality** (unencrypted data is exposed).
    -   **Protection:** **Encryption** (makes captured data unreadable) and avoiding sensitive activities on insecure public networks.

### :violet[Network Attack Summary]

| Attack                 | What It Does                             | CIA Principle(s) Threatened            |
| :--------------------- | :--------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------- |
| **Denial of Service**  | Floods a service until it goes offline   | **Availability**                       |
| **Man-in-the-Middle**  | Intercepts and alters communication      | **Confidentiality** and **Integrity**  |
| **DNS Attack**         | Redirects users to a fake website        | **Confidentiality** and **Integrity**  |
| **Packet Sniffing**    | Captures data travelling on the network  | **Confidentiality**                    |

### ✅ Key Takeaways

-   **Network-based attacks** target the connections and infrastructure that enable digital communication.
-   **Denial of Service** attacks primarily threaten **Availability**.
-   **Man-in-the-Middle** and **DNS attacks** compromise both **Confidentiality** and **Integrity**.
-   **Packet sniffing** directly breaches **Confidentiality**.
-   Crucial protections include **HTTPS**, **encryption**, and caution on public networks.

## :blue[🛡️ Introduction to Security Controls]

This topic introduces **security controls** as the primary tools for defense, explaining their purpose in risk reduction and how they are managed through **defense in depth**.

### :green[Security Control Definition]

**:green[Definition:]** Any measure or means implemented to protect assets and reduce risk. It's any step taken to make an attacker's task more difficult.
-   :yellow[**Examples:**] A password on your phone, a lock on a door, an employee not opening unfamiliar links.
-   **Purpose:** Controls maintain the CIA Triad (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability) by preventing unauthorized access, detecting harmful activity, and supporting recovery.

### :green[Purpose of Controls]

-   The central purpose is to protect **assets** (data, devices, software, networks, people) from **threats** (malware, phishing, network attacks).
-   Controls act as a barrier between valuable assets and potential dangers.

### :green[Risk Reduction]

-   Controls reduce **risk** by closing **vulnerabilities**.
-   **:orange[Key Idea:]** The goal is not to eliminate risk entirely (which is impossible) but to reduce it to the lowest reasonably achievable level. This is **risk reduction**.

### :green[Security Management and Defense in Depth]

-   **Security Management:** The organized process of choosing, applying, monitoring, and updating security controls based on asset importance and risk assessment.
-   **Defense in Depth:** A central principle meaning **layered protection**. Instead of relying on a single control, multiple layers are placed one behind another. If one layer fails, the next one stops the attacker.
    -   :yellow[**Analogy:**] An old castle with a moat, high wall, guards, and an iron gate. Each is a layer of defense.

### ✅ Key Takeaways

-   **Security controls** are measures to protect assets and reduce risk.
-   Their primary goal is to maintain the **CIA Triad**.
-   Controls aim for **risk reduction**, acknowledging that perfect security is unattainable.
-   **Security management** is the structured approach to deploying controls.
-   **Defense in depth** employs multiple, layered controls to provide comprehensive protection.

---

## :blue[📋 Administrative Controls]

**Administrative controls** are the first type of security control, focusing on people and rules to guide employee behavior and reduce human error.

### :green[Administrative Controls Definition]

**:green[Definition:]** Security controls that focus on laws, rules, policies, procedures, and training to organize behavior and protect assets. They don't involve programs or devices directly.

### :violet[Key Administrative Controls]

1.  ### :green[Policy]
    -   **What it Does:** A general rule stating what is required or not permitted within an organization. It sets the goal.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] "All employees must use strong passwords."

2.  ### :green[Procedure]
    -   **What it Does:** A detailed set of steps explaining exactly how a policy is to be carried out. It provides the method.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] "To report a suspicious email: 1. Do not click links. 2. Forward to security. 3. Delete it."
    -   **:orange[Distinction:]** **Policy = What** is required; **Procedure = How** it is done.

3.  ### :green[Security Awareness Training]
    -   **What it Does:** Instruction for employees to learn how to recognize threats (e.g., social engineering, phishing) and respond correctly.
    -   **Role:** Directly addresses human vulnerabilities, which are often the weakest link in security.
    -   **:orange[Effectiveness:]** Most effective when continuous (regular sessions, simulated phishing) as threats evolve.

4.  ### :green[Standard]
    -   **What it Does:** A specific, mandatory requirement that converts a general policy into a precise condition. It removes ambiguity.
    -   :yellow[**Example (for "strong passwords" policy):**] "Passwords must be no fewer than twelve characters and must contain numbers and symbols."
    -   **:orange[Relationship:]** Policy sets the goal, Standard sets the precise condition, Procedure sets the steps.

### :violet[Administrative Controls Summary]

| Control                 | What It Does                           | Example                                         |
| :---------------------- | :------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------- |
| **Policy**              | States the general requirement         | Passwords must be strong                        |
| **Procedure**           | Gives the detailed steps               | How to report a suspicious email                |
| **Security Awareness Training** | Teaches people to recognize threats    | Spotting a phishing message                     |
| **Standard**            | Sets a precise, mandatory condition    | Passwords need 12+ characters, numbers, symbols |

### ✅ Key Takeaways

-   **Administrative controls** focus on managing people's behavior and establishing rules.
-   **Policies** set general requirements; **procedures** detail the steps to meet them.
-   **Security awareness training** is vital for empowering employees to recognize and counter threats.
-   **Standards** provide precise, mandatory conditions to enforce policies effectively.
-   These controls form the foundational layer of defense by reducing human errors.

---

## :blue[💻 Technical Controls]

**Technical controls** are security measures that use technology (tools and programs) to protect systems and data, forming a crucial line of defense against cyberattacks.

### :green[Technical Controls Definition]

**:green[Definition:]** Cybersecurity measures implemented through hardware or software to protect systems by technological means. They directly stand in the path of an attacker.

### :violet[Key Technical Controls]

1.  ### :green[Firewall]
    -   **Function:** Controls network connections, acting as a guard at the network boundary. It decides which connections (traffic) are permitted to enter or leave and blocks suspicious ones.
    -   **Defends Against:** Harmful network connections, unauthorized access.

2.  ### :green[Antivirus]
    -   **Function:** Detects and removes known malware (viruses, Trojans, spyware) from a device by scanning files.
    -   **Defends Against:** Malware infections. Must be updated continually to recognize new threats.

3.  ### :green[Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR)]
    -   **Function:** An advanced system installed on endpoint devices (laptops, phones) that continuously monitors device *behavior* for unusual activity, even from unknown programs. It provides investigation and response capabilities, like isolating an infected device.
    -   **Defends Against:** New, sophisticated, and unknown threats by focusing on behavioral anomalies.
    -   **:orange[Distinction from Antivirus:]** EDR goes beyond just detecting known malware; it monitors behavior and actively responds.

4.  ### :green[Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)]
    -   **Function:** Strengthens authentication by requiring a second proof of identity beyond a password (e.g., a code sent to your phone, a fingerprint scan).
    -   **Defends Against:** Stolen or guessed passwords. Even if a hacker has your password, they can't log in without the second factor.

5.  ### :green[Security Information and Event Management (SIEM)]
    -   **Function:** A system that collects and analyzes security records (logs and events) from *all* devices and tools across an organization into one central place. It links these events to detect patterns indicative of an attack that might be missed individually.
    -   **Defends Against:** Hidden, coordinated, and complex attacks by providing a complete picture of security events. It supports human analysts.

### :violet[Technical Controls Summary]

| Control       | Function                                    | Defends Against                                   |
| :------------ | :------------------------------------------ | :------------------------------------------------ |
| **Firewall**  | Controls connections at the network boundary | Harmful network connections                       |
| **Antivirus** | Detects and removes malware                  | Viruses, Trojans, spyware                         |
| **EDR**       | Watches device behavior and responds        | New and unknown threats, active compromises       |
| **MFA**       | Adds a second proof of identity             | Stolen passwords, unauthorized account access     |
| **SIEM**      | Collects and analyses events centrally      | Hidden, coordinated attacks, provides overall view |

### ✅ Key Takeaways

-   **Technical controls** are technological tools that provide automated protection against threats.
-   **Firewalls** protect network boundaries; **Antivirus** targets known malware.
-   **EDR** offers advanced, behavioral-based protection and response for endpoints.
-   **MFA** significantly enhances account security beyond just passwords.
-   **SIEM** centralizes and analyzes security events to detect complex attacks.
-   These controls are most effective when combined with administrative and physical controls.

---

## :blue[🔒 Physical Controls]

**Physical controls** are security measures that protect tangible objects, facilities, and equipment. They are essential because physical access can bypass digital protections.

### :green[Physical Controls Definition]

**:green[Definition:]** Security measures that protect physical assets, such as buildings, equipment, and access to secure areas, from unauthorized access or environmental damage.

### :violet[Key Physical Controls]

1.  ### :green[Security Guards]
    -   **Function:** Human personnel who monitor entrances, verify identities, and prevent unauthorized entry. They can make real-time judgments and respond immediately to unusual situations.
    -   **Role:** Provide active human deterrence and response.

2.  ### :green[CCTV (Closed-Circuit Television)]
    -   **Function:** Cameras that record activity in specific areas.
    -   **Purpose:**
        -   **Deterrence:** Visible cameras discourage wrongdoing.
        -   **Evidence:** Recordings provide proof of events, supporting **accountability**.
    -   **Role:** Continuous surveillance and evidence collection.

3.  ### :green[Locks]
    -   **Function:** Devices that secure doors to buildings, server rooms, and valuable equipment. They prevent physical access.
    -   **Role:** A fundamental and traditional control directly preventing unauthorized entry, similar to how passwords protect digital data (**Confidentiality**).

4.  ### :green[Access Badges]
    -   **Function:** Employee cards that grant or deny entry to specific areas. They are held near a reader, and the door opens only if the holder is authorized.
    -   **Role:** Enforces **authorization** in the physical world and provides **accountability** by recording who entered where and when.

### :violet[Physical Controls Summary]

| Control           | What It Does                      | Related Principles                        |
| :---------------- | :-------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------- |
| **Security Guards** | Human judgement at the entrance   | Prevention and immediate response         |
| **CCTV**          | Records activity and deters       | **Accountability**                        |
| **Locks**         | Prevent physical access to devices | **Confidentiality** (of physical data)    |
| **Access Badges** | Control who may enter which place | **Authorization** and **Accountability**  |

### ✅ Key Takeaways

-   **Physical controls** protect tangible assets and facilities, preventing direct access to systems and data.
-   **Security guards** offer dynamic human oversight and response.
-   **CCTV** provides deterrence and crucial evidence for **accountability**.
-   **Locks** are fundamental barriers against physical intrusion.
-   **Access badges** manage **authorization** for entry and track movements, supporting **accountability**.
-   Physical security is an indispensable part of a comprehensive cybersecurity strategy.

## :blue[⚙️ Control Functions]

Beyond classifying controls by type (administrative, technical, physical), they can also be classified by *what they do* and *when they act* relative to an attack.

### :violet[Five Control Functions]

1.  ### :green[Preventive Control]
    -   **Timing:** Acts **before** an attack occurs.
    -   **Purpose:** To stop an attack from happening at all.
    -   :yellow[**Examples:**] Locks, firewalls, Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA), security awareness training.
    -   **:orange[Goal:]** Prevention is always preferred.

2.  ### :green[Detective Control]
    -   **Timing:** Acts **during** or **after** an attack has occurred.
    -   **Purpose:** To detect that an attack is taking place or has taken place and provide warning. It reveals the problem.
    -   :yellow[**Examples:**] CCTV cameras, security alerts, system logs, SIEM (Security Information and Event Management).
    -   **:orange[Goal:]** Allows defenders to catch attackers at any stage of the attack lifecycle.

3.  ### :green[Corrective Control]
    -   **Timing:** Acts **after** an attack has been detected.
    -   **Purpose:** To stop the damage, contain the incident, and repair the immediate problem.
    -   :yellow[**Examples:**] Removing malware from a device, isolating an infected device from the network, closing a compromised account.
    -   **:orange[Distinction:]** Corrective *stops the harm*; Recovery *restores after the harm*.

4.  ### :green[Recovery Control]
    -   **Timing:** Acts **after** an attack has been stopped.
    -   **Purpose:** To restore systems and data to their normal, working state. Connects to the **Availability** principle.
    -   :yellow[**Examples:**] Restoring data from a backup, rebuilding compromised servers, re-establishing network services.
    -   **:orange[Distinction:]** Corrective deals with the incident; Recovery deals with the aftermath and restoration.

5.  ### :green[Compensating Control]
    -   **Timing:** Used **when an ideal control cannot be applied** (e.g., due to technical limitations or cost).
    -   **Purpose:** An alternative measure or substitute that makes up for the absence of a primary control, reducing risk until a better solution is possible.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] If an old system cannot support MFA, increased monitoring (a detective control) and stricter access limitations serve as compensating controls.

### :violet[Control Functions Summary]

| Function         | Timing                | Purpose                                      | Example                                     |
| :--------------- | :-------------------- | :------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------ |
| **Preventive**   | Before the attack     | Stops the attack from happening              | Firewall, MFA, training                     |
| **Detective**    | During/after attack   | Detects and warns of an attack               | CCTV, SIEM, alerts, logs                    |
| **Corrective**   | After detection       | Stops damage, fixes immediate problem        | Removing malware, isolating device          |
| **Recovery**     | After the attack      | Restores systems and data to normal state    | Restoring from a backup                     |
| **Compensating** | When ideal unavailable | Alternative measure to reduce risk           | Extra monitoring for an unpatchable system |

### ✅ Key Takeaways

-   Controls are classified by their **function** based on *when they act* relative to an attack.
-   **Preventive controls** aim to stop attacks before they start.
-   **Detective controls** identify attacks in progress or after they've occurred.
-   **Corrective controls** mitigate damage and fix immediate issues.
-   **Recovery controls** restore operations and data after an incident.
-   **Compensating controls** are temporary substitutes when ideal controls aren't feasible.

## :blue[📡 Monitoring and Detection]

Effective defense relies on actively observing systems to identify suspicious activity. This topic explains the practical steps of how detection works, from recording events to identifying genuine threats.

### :violet[Four Steps of Detection]

1.  ### :green[Logging]
    -   **What it Does:** The recording of every event that occurs within a system into a chronological record called a **log**. This includes who accessed what, when, and what actions were taken.
    -   **:orange[Foundation:]** Logs are the raw material for all detection. They are the practical application of **accountability**.
    -   **:gray[Challenge:]** Logs are enormous and require automated analysis.

2.  ### :green[Alerting]
    -   **What it Does:** The automatic notification a system sends when it finds something suspicious within the logs.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] If an account tries to log in incorrectly 50 times in one minute, an alert is triggered.
    -   :yellow[**Analogy:**] A fire alarm that sounds automatically when danger is sensed.
    -   **Role:** Draws attention to unusual events, signaling where security personnel should investigate.

3.  ### :green[Security Monitoring]
    -   **What it Does:** The continuous, around-the-clock observation of systems and alerts.
    -   **Purpose:** Ensures that attacks are noticed at any time, as attackers do not rest.
    -   :yellow[**Analogy:**] A guard in a control room watching multiple screens for any unusual movement.

4.  ### :green[Threat Detection]
    -   **What it Does:** The analysis of alerts and data to distinguish a **genuine threat** requiring action from a **false alarm** (ordinary error).
    -   **Purpose:** Avoids wasting resources on non-threats while ensuring real attacks are not missed.
    -   :yellow[**Analogy:**] A doctor examining test results to distinguish normal variations from urgent medical conditions.

### :violet[Detection Workflow Summary]

| Step                 | What It Does                                 | Comparison                           |
| :------------------- | :------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------- |
| **Logging**          | Records every event                          | Keeping a written record             |
| **Alerting**         | Signals suspicious events automatically      | A fire alarm                         |
| **Security Monitoring** | Watches continuously, around the clock       | A guard in a control room            |
| **Threat Detection** | Separates real threats from false alarms     | A doctor reading test results        |

### ✅ Key Takeaways

-   Detection works through a sequential process: **logging** records events.
-   **Alerting** automatically flags suspicious activities in logs.
-   **Security monitoring** involves continuous, 24/7 observation of systems and alerts.
-   **Threat detection** is the crucial analysis that differentiates genuine threats from false alarms.
-   These steps are fundamental to identifying and responding to cyberattacks in a timely manner.

## :blue[🖥️ Security Operations Center (SOC)]

The **Security Operations Center (SOC)** is the central hub where monitoring, detection, and response to cyber threats are carried out by a dedicated team around the clock.

### :green[SOC Purpose]

**:green[Definition:]** A centralized team and facility responsible for continuously monitoring, detecting, and responding to cyber threats and incidents.
-   **:orange[Role:]** It's where the goals of protecting, detecting, and responding are put into practice 24/7.
-   **Tools Used:** The **SIEM** (Security Information and Event Management) is a primary tool, collecting and analyzing events. Other tools include EDR, threat intelligence platforms, and vulnerability management tools.

### :green[SOC Workflow]

The SOC operates through a defined sequence of steps:

1.  ### :green[Monitoring]
    -   **Action:** The team continuously watches security screens, alerts, and logs for any suspicious activity.

2.  ### :green[Detection]
    -   **Action:** An alert is raised by a system (e.g., SIEM) about something unusual or suspicious, drawing the team's attention.

3.  ### :green[Investigation]
    -   **Action:** The team examines the alert to determine if it is a genuine threat requiring action or a false alarm. This involves analyzing data and logs.

4.  ### :green[Response]
    -   **Action:** If a genuine threat is confirmed, the team acts immediately to stop the attack, contain its spread, and limit damage (e.g., isolating infected systems, blocking malicious IPs).

5.  ### :green[Recovery]
    -   **Action:** After the attack is stopped, systems are restored to their normal state, and lessons learned from the incident are used to improve future defenses (e.g., updating detection rules, patching vulnerabilities).

### :violet[SOC Workflow Summary]

| Step             | Action                                      |
| :--------------- | :------------------------------------------ |
| **Monitoring**   | Continuously watch screens and logs         |
| **Detection**    | Notice an alert about something suspicious  |
| **Investigation** | Decide whether the alert is a real threat   |
| **Response**     | Stop the attack and limit the damage        |
| **Recovery**     | Restore systems and learn from the event    |

### :green[Security Analyst Responsibilities]

A **security analyst** is typically the first line of defense within a SOC. Their responsibilities include:
-   Continuously watching screens and alerts.
-   Sorting alerts to prioritize genuine threats over false alarms.
-   Investigating important alerts to understand what is happening.
-   Escalating large or complex attacks to specialized teams (e.g., incident response).
-   Documenting incidents and contributing to improving detection rules.

### ✅ Key Takeaways

-   The **SOC** is the central hub for 24/7 monitoring, detection, and response to cyber threats.
-   Its workflow follows a sequence: **Monitoring → Detection → Investigation → Response → Recovery**.
-   **Security analysts** are critical SOC personnel who triage alerts and investigate incidents.
-   The SOC integrates administrative, technical, and human elements to provide a comprehensive defense.
