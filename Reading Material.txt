Living in a Digital World
By the end of this topic, the learner should be able to:

Define digital transformation and describe how everyday services have moved from manual processes to digital ones.
Explain what a connected system is and trace how devices exchange information over the internet.
Identify what a digital asset is and recognise common examples of personal digital assets.
Distinguish between personal data and organizational data and justify why each must be protected.
What Does “Digital World” Mean?


A digital world is a society where people, organizations, and services use computers, networks, and the internet to communicate, work, learn, and access information.(1)

Digital Transformation
Digital Transformation means businesses and people are changing how they do things using technology. Instead of old ways, they use computers and the internet(2)
Examples
Schools: Previously, teachers wrote on chalkboards, and students used printed textbooks. Now, many schools use Google Classroom, online learning platforms, and digital homework instead.
Banks: Previously, go to the bank, talk to tellers, get cash. Now, Mobile apps, online transfers, ATMs, digital wallets
Shopping: Previously, go to the store, talk to the cashier. Now, Amazon, online shopping, home delivery
Healthcare: Previously, Paper patient records. Now, digital health records, telemedicine (doctor visits by video)
Why does it matter?
Everything is becoming digital. Learning to work with technology is essential for any career

Connected Systems
What is it?
Connected systems are devices and services that work together through the internet. They talk to each other and share information automatically(3)
Examples
Smart Home: your phone controls lights, temperature, door locks, devices talk to each other
Your Phone: Connects to WiFi, cellular network, social media, email, all connected at once, share photos instantly to cloud storage
Modern Cars: GPS for navigation, bluetooth for phone calls, connected to manufacturer for updates and diagnostics
Smartwatch: Connects to phone, Tracks your fitness, sends your notifications
When devices connect, they can do more together than alone, but more connected devices can increase security risks, so it is important to use strong passwords and keep systems updated(4)
Digital Assets
What is it?
Digital assets are things of value that exist only online. You can’t hold them, but they are worth real money or important to you(1)
Types of Digital Assets
Personal Digital Assets: photos and videos you’ve taken, messages and emails, social media accounts (Instagram, TikTok, Discord), games and in-game items, cryptocurrency/digital money, documents you’ve created
Business Digital Assets: Company database and customer information, software and apps, websites, brand and logo designs, business plans and strategies, employee records
Gaming Digital Assets: Game accounts, virtual items and skins, virtual rewards/items
Why to Protect them?
If hackers steal your social media, they can impersonate you

If they steal company data, the business loses money

Your digital assets are worth protecting just like physical property(1)

Gaming account with rare items worth $500, Hacker steals account, Account is gone forever, Loss of $500

Personal and Organizational Data
What is Data?
Data is information about people and organizations, it can be(2)

Numbers (age, salary, grades)
Text (names, addresses, passwords)
Images (photos, fingerprints)
Videos
Location information
Personal Data (Your Information)
Identifying Information: Name, age, date of birth, phone number email address, home address social security number
Behavioral Data: What websites you visit, what you search for, what videos you watch, where you go (location), What you buy
Health Data: Doctor visits, medications, allergies, mental health information
Financial Data: Bank account numbers, credit card numbers, salary information, loan information
Organizational Data (Business Information)
Customer Information and purchase history, employee records and payroll, financial reports, business secrets and strategies, product designs(5)
Why does it matter?
Business use your data to: improve services, personalize user experiences, protect against fraud, and understand customer needs
Hackers steal data to: commit identity theft (pretend to be you), commit fraud (use your credit card), sell to criminals, blackmail people, disrupt businesses
How to protect your data
Use strong passwords
Don’t share personal information online
Be careful what you post on social media
Don’t click suspicious links
Keep software updated(4)

Topic 2
What is Cybersecurity
By the end of this topic, the learner should be able to:

State a clear definition of cybersecurity and explain the meaning of its two parts.
Describe the three goals that every protection system aims to achieve.
Explain why protection depends on people, processes, and technology together.
Distinguish cybersecurity from information security and describe how the two are related.
Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting computers, networks, and data from being stolen, damaged, or misused by hackers and cybercriminals.


Think of it like this:
Your home has locks on doors and windows = Physical security
Your computer needs protection from hackers = Cybersecurity
What Cybersecurity Protects:
Your personal information (passwords, photos, messages)
Business data (customer records, financial information)
Critical infrastructure (hospitals, power plants, banks)
Devices (computers, phones, tablets, smart devices)
Real-World Example: A hacker tries to steal your gaming account. Cybersecurity protects you by:

Checking your password is strong
Using two-factor authentication (extra verification step)
Monitoring for suspicious login attempts
Alerting you if something unusual happens

What are Security Goals? Security goals are the three main things cybersecurity tries to accomplish.

Goal 1: Confidentiality - Keep Secrets Secret
What it means: Only people who should have access to information can see it. Everyone else is blocked from viewing it.

Real Examples:
Medical Records:

Your doctor can see your health information
Your friends cannot see it
Random hackers definitely cannot see it
How to Protect Confidentiality:
Use passwords
Use encryption (turning information into code)
Use privacy settings
Only share information with people who need to know
Goal 2: Integrity - Keep Information Accurate
What it means: Information cannot be changed or corrupted without permission. What you see is exactly what was intended.

Real Examples:
Medicine Dosage:

Doctor prescribes 10mg of medicine
Hacker changes it to 100mg
Patient gets harmed because information changed
How to Protect Integrity:
Use digital signatures (proof that something wasn't changed)
Use checksums (mathematical checks)
Keep backups of important files
Verify information hasn't been altered
Goal 3: Availability - Keep Systems Running
What it means: Systems and information must work when you need them. They shouldn't be down or unavailable.

Real Examples:
Hospital Systems:

Doctors need patient records to help patients
If system is down, they can't get information
Critical for patient safety
How to Protect Availability:
Keep systems updated and working
Have backup systems ready
Backup important data
Prevent attacks that shut systems down

What does this mean? Security isn't just about technology. You also need to protect the people who use it and the processes they follow.

1. Protecting People
The Problem: People are often the weakest link to security. Humans make mistakes, fall for tricks, and can be careless.

Common Mistakes People Make:
Using weak passwords like "password123"
Clicking suspicious links in emails
Opening attachments from unknown senders
Sharing passwords with friends
Using public WiFi without protection
Downloading files from untrusted sources
How to Protect People:
Train people on cybersecurity
Teach them to recognize phishing emails (fake emails trying to trick you)
Make them aware of social engineering (manipulating people to give information)
Create a culture where everyone cares about security
Report suspicious activity
2. Protecting Processes
What are the Processes? Processes are the steps and procedures people follow to keep systems secure.

Examples of Security Processes:
Password Management Process:

Create strong passwords
Change passwords regularly
Never write passwords down
Use different passwords for different accounts
Access Control Process:

Only give people access they need for their job
Remove access when people leave
Check regularly that access is still appropriate
Get approval before giving access
Incident Response Process:

If a breach happens, follow specific steps
Contact the incident response team
Isolate affected systems
Investigate what happened
Tell affected people
How to Protect Through Processes:
Document procedures clearly
Train people on the procedures
Check that procedures are being followed
Update procedures when things change
Practice the procedures regularly
3. Protecting Technology
What is Technology Protection? Using technical tools and systems to defend against hackers and malware.

Examples of Technology Protection:
Firewalls:

Control what information enters and leaves your network
Like a bouncer at a club - only lets good traffic through
Antivirus Software:

Scans for malware and viruses
Removes them if found
Encryption:

Turns information into code so hackers can't read it
Even if they steal it, they can't understand it
Two-Factor Authentication:

Need two different proofs you're you (password + phone code)
Much harder for hackers to break through
Backups:

Copy of all important data stored separately
If system is hacked, you still have the original data
Why All Three Matter Together:
Imagine a bank:

Technology: Has strong firewalls and encryption
Process: Has access controls and procedures
People: Employees trained on security
If anyone is missing:

No people training: Employee falls for phishing, hacker gets in
No good processes: Anyone can access customer data
No good technology: Hacker breaks through the firewall
You need all three to work together!

Are They the Same Thing? Not exactly! They're related but different.

Information Security (Broader)
Definition: Protecting ALL information - digital and physical - from being stolen, damaged, or misused.

What it includes:
Digital information (emails, databases, files)
Physical information (paper documents, printed files)
Keeping information accurate
Keeping information available
Managing all types of information risks
Real Examples:
Locking filing cabinets with important documents
Keeping background checks confidential
Shredding old documents before throwing away
Cybersecurity (Narrower)
Definition: Protecting specifically DIGITAL systems, networks, and data from cyber-attacks and online threats.

What it includes:
Protecting against hackers
Protecting against malware and viruses
Defending against online attacks
Securing digital devices and systems
Protecting digital data
Real Examples:
Using firewalls to block hackers
Installing antivirus software
Using strong passwords
Comparison:

Key Difference:
Information Security = Protecting ALL types of information Cybersecurity = Protecting DIGITAL information from cyber attacks


Concept	Definition	Example
Cybersecurity	Protecting digital systems from hackers	Using passwords and antivirus software
Confidentiality	Keep information secret	Only you see your personal photos
Integrity	Keep information accurate	Bank balance shows correct amount
Availability	Keep systems working	Website is accessible when you need it
Protecting People	Train people on security	Teaching staff to spot phishing emails
Protecting Processes	Follow security procedures	Change passwords every 90 days
Protecting Technology	Use security tools	Install firewalls and antivirus
Information Security	Protect all information types	Digital and physical protection
Cybersecurity	Protect digital systems only	Protection against online attacks

Topic 4
Core Security Principles: The CIA Triad
By the end of this topic, the learner should be able to:

Define confidentiality and give an example of how it is maintained and how it can be broken.
Define integrity and explain what it means for information to remain unaltered.
Define availability and explain why information must be accessible when needed.
Explain why the three principles are studied together as a single triad.

The CIA Triad is the three most important rules of cybersecurity. CIA stands for:
Confidentiality
Integrity
Availability
These three principles work together like the three legs of a stool. If one breaks, the whole security system fails.(1)


Every security decision in cybersecurity is based on protecting one or more of these three principles.(1)


Confidentiality means keeping information private and secret. Only people who are supposed to see it can see it. Everyone else is blocked.(1)

Why Confidentiality Matters:
What Needs to Be Kept Confidential:
Your Personal Information:

Passwords and PIN numbers
Social Security number
Medical records
Financial account numbers
Private messages and emails
Photos you don't want shared
Business Information:

Customer databases
Trade secrets and formulas
Financial records
Strategic plans
Employee salaries
Research and development projects
Real Examples of Confidentiality:
Medical Records:
Hospital Setting:

Your doctor can see your medical history
Nurses caring for you can see relevant information
Random person walking by cannot access it
This is confidentiality protected
If Confidentiality Fails:

Hacker steals your medical records
Finds out you have a mental health condition
Sells information to employers
Employer discriminates against you
Your private medical information is now public
How to Protect Confidentiality:
Use Passwords:
Create strong, unique passwords
Don't share passwords with others
Change passwords if you think they're compromised
Use different passwords for different accounts
Encryption:
Turns information into secret code
Only person with the key can read it
Even if hacker steals it, they can't read it
Example:

Original message: "My password is SecurePass123"
Encrypted: "kJ#9mL@2pQ$5vX8wR"
Hacker sees "kJ#9mL@2pQ$5vX8wR" - can't understand it
Privacy Settings:
Set social media to private
Control who can see your information
Don't share sensitive information publicly
Be careful what you post online
Access Control:
Limit who can see information
Give access only to people who need it for their job
Remove access when people leave
Secure Communication:
Use encrypted messaging apps
Avoid sending sensitive info on unsecured WiFi
Use HTTPS websites (notice the "S" in https)

Integrity means information stays accurate and hasn't been changed or corrupted. What you see is exactly what it's supposed to be.(2)

Why Integrity Matters:
What Needs Integrity Protection:
Medical Records:

Doctor prescribes 10mg of medicine
Must stay 10mg in the system
If hacker changes to 100mg, patient gets harmed
Real Examples of Integrity Failures:
Hospital Drug Dosage:
Scenario:

Doctor prescribes 50mg of pain medication
Hacker breaks into hospital system
Changes prescription to 500mg
Nurse follows the system (doesn't know it's changed)
Patient gets 10x the intended dose
Patient suffers serious harm or dies
Integrity failure causes real-world harm
Financial Transaction:
Scenario:

You transfer $100 to a friend
Hacker intercepts the transaction
Changes amount to $1,000
Your account loses $1,000 instead of $100
Friend receives $1,000 instead of $100
Financial records are now wrong
Integrity failure causes financial fraud
College Application:
Scenario:

Student's SAT scores recorded as 1500 (perfect)
Hacker breaks in and changes to 600
School sees low score
Student doesn't get accepted
Integrity failure ruins student's future
How to Protect Integrity:
Digital Signatures:
Proof that information came from the right person
Proves it hasn't been changed
Works like a fingerprint for documents
Checksums:
Mathematical calculation of data
If data changes even slightly, checksum changes
Can detect if data was altered
Backups:
Keep copies of important data
If data is corrupted, restore from backup
Regular backups ensure you have current copies
Version Control:
Keep track of changes to documents
Know who changed what and when
Can revert to previous versions if needed
Audit Trails:
Record who accessed what information
Record what changes were made
Track everything for accountability
Verification:
Double-check important information
Confirm changes are legitimate
Don't trust suspicious modifications

The Balance:
Sometimes protecting one principle means making a trade-off with another:(1)

Example 1: Security vs Speed

Strong encryption (confidentiality) = slow performance (availability)
Need to find balance
Example 2: Security vs Convenience

Complex password policy (confidentiality) = harder for users
Need to find balance
Example 3: Data Collection vs Privacy

Monitoring for attacks (availability/integrity) = less privacy
Need to find balance
Real-World Example - Bank:
Bank needs all three:

Confidentiality: Customer data encrypted, access restricted
Integrity: Transactions accurate, balances correct
Availability: Banking systems running 24/7
If bank loses one:

Lose confidentiality: Customer data stolen (customers panic, leave bank)
Lose integrity: Wrong balances (customers distrust bank, leave)
Lose availability: Can't do banking (customers can't access accounts)
Banks must protect all three equally!

Real-World Example – Email System:

Email system needs all three:

Confidentiality: Emails encrypted, only authorized users can read messages.
Integrity: Email content remains unchanged during transmission.
Availability: Email service accessible anytime for sending and receiving messages.
If the email system loses one:
Lose confidentiality: Sensitive emails are intercepted or read by unauthorized people (privacy breached, users lose trust).
Lose integrity: Email contents are altered, attachments modified, or fake emails are inserted (miscommunication, fraud, and phishing risks increase).
Lose availability: Users cannot send or receive emails due to server outages (communication stops, business operations are disrupted).
Email systems must protect all three equally!

Integrity	Keep accurate	Data accuracy	Digital signatures, backups, checksums	Bank balance shows correct amount
Availability	Keep working	System access	Redundancy, backups, monitoring	Website works 24/7

Topic 5
Additional Security Concepts
By the end of this topic, the learner should be able to:

Define authentication and describe how a system confirms a user’s identity.
Define authorization and distinguish it clearly from authentication.
Define accountability and explain how recorded actions support security.
Define non-repudiation and explain how it prevents a person from denying an action.

The CIA Triad is the foundation of cybersecurity, but there are four more important principles that work together with it. These principles answer four critical questions:(1)

Authentication: "Are you really who you say you are?"
Authorization: "What are you allowed to do?"
Accountability: "What did you do?"
Non-Repudiation: "Can you deny you did it?"

Authentication is proving your identity. It answers the question: "Are you really who you say you are?"(1)

Think of it like showing your ID to prove you're you.

Real-World Examples of Authentication:
At the Airport:
You show your ID to prove you're you
Agent checks photo matches your face
You're authenticated (proven to be you)
You can board the plane
At a Store:
Cashier asks for ID when you buy age-restricted items
You prove you're over 21
You're authenticated
You can make the purchase
How Authentication Works Online:
Password Authentication:
Simple Process:

You enter your username
You enter your password
System checks if password is correct
If correct: You're authenticated (you can log in)
If wrong: You're not authenticated (denied access)
Example - Social Media Login:

You enter username: "john_smith"
You enter password: "MyP@ssw0rd123"
System verifies password is correct
Access granted to your account
Problem with Passwords Alone:
Passwords can be guessed
Passwords can be stolen
Passwords can be hacked
Not always secure enough
Types of Authentication Methods:
Something You Know (Knowledge):
Passwords you remember
PIN numbers (Personal ID Number)
Security questions ("What's your mother's maiden name?")
Passphrases (longer than passwords)
Pros:

Easy to use

Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA):
What is it? Using multiple authentication methods together. Even stronger security.(2)

Authorization means determining what someone is allowed to do after they've proven who they are. It answers: "Now that I know you're you, what access should I give you?"(1)

How Authentication and Authorization Work Together:

Real-World Example:

Golden Rule: Give people the MINIMUM access they need to do their job.(2)

If account gets hacked, damage is limited
Prevents accidental access to sensitive data
Reduces risk of insider threats

CANNOT see medical records

Limited damage

Non-repudiation means you cannot deny you did something. There's proof it was you.(1)

It answers: "Can you deny you did this action?"

Both parties cannot deny it
Legally binding
Court will enforce it

You can prove hacker did it

Before hackers can attack something, they need to know what exists. In this topic, we'll learn what assets are, what makes a good attack target, and how your digital footprint exposes you to attacks.

Threats, Vulnerabilities, Risks, and Attacks
By the end of this topic, the learner should be able to:

These four concepts are foundational to cybersecurity, but they're often confused. Let's define each one clearly and understand how they relate to each other.

Think of it like home security:

Threat: A burglar wanting to rob your house
Vulnerability: An unlocked window or door
Risk: The possibility that the burglar will successfully rob you
Attack: The burglar actually breaking in and stealing
Let's explore this in detail.

What is a Threat?

A threat is anything that could potentially harm your systems, data, or organization. It's the danger itself.(1)

Key Idea: A threat exists whether or not you have defenses against it. The threat is about the attacker's intent and capability.(2)

What is a Risk?

A risk is the probability and impact of a threat exploiting a vulnerability. It's the likelihood that something bad will happen combined with the damage it would cause.(1)

Impact describes the damage after an attack succeeds, while risk combines both the likelihood of the attack and its potential impact.

Key Idea: Risk depends on THREE things:(4)

Is there a threat? (Someone wanting to attack)
Is there a vulnerability? (A weakness to exploit)
What's the impact? (How much damage would it cause)
Risk Formula:


Understanding the Formula:
Example 1: High Risk

Threat: Yes (hackers actively targeting hospitals)
Vulnerability: Yes (outdated unpatched systems)
Impact: High (patient safety at risk, lives endangered)
Result: HIGH RISK - Must be addressed immediately
Example 2: No Risk

Threat: Yes (hackers active)
Vulnerability: No (systems fully patched, security strong)
Impact: High (if it happened)
Result: NO RISK - Vulnerability doesn't exist, so threat can't succeed
Example 3: Low Risk

Threat: Yes (hackers exist)
Vulnerability: Yes (outdated system)
Impact: Low (contains only public information)
Result: LOW RISK - Even if compromised, limited damage

Did You Know? A Bank's Defenses
A bank faces a constant threat from criminals who want to steal money, but that threat only becomes dangerous when weaknesses exist, such as weak passwords or no firewalls. By adding strong firewalls, encryption, and staff training, the bank removes those weaknesses, so even though the threat still exists, an attack is far less likely to succeed.

WannaCry Ransomware Attack (2017)
Threat:
Cybercriminals spreading ransomware to lock files and demand money.
Vulnerability:
Many computers were running outdated software that had not been updated.
Attack:
The ransomware spread across networks, locked files on unpatched computers, and demanded payment to unlock them, reaching more than 200 countries.
Result:
Hundreds of thousands of computers were locked, hospitals and businesses were disrupted, and losses reached billions of dollars.
Lesson Learned:
Keeping software updated could have prevented most of the damage.
Did You Know? Target Data Breach (2013)
Threat: Cybercriminals wanting to steal credit card data.
Vulnerability: A supplier with access to Target's network was not properly secured.
Attack: The attackers got in through the supplier, reached the payment systems, and stole credit card details.
Result: About 40 million credit cards were stolen and customer trust was badly damaged.
Lesson Learned: Organizations must control and monitor the access they give to outside suppliers.
Did You Know? Insider Threat and Corporate Espionage
Threat: A competitor or foreign group wanting to steal trade secrets.
Vulnerability: A trusted employee had access to secret files with little monitoring.
Attack: The employee was paid to copy confidential files and send them outside the company.
Result: Valuable trade secrets were lost and the company lost its competitive advantage.
Lesson Learned: Monitoring access and having clear exit procedures help catch insider threats.

Did You Know? The Same Pattern Fits Every Asset
The threat, vulnerability, risk, and attack relationship applies to every type of asset. With data, a hacker (threat) targets unencrypted files (vulnerability), the risk is that the data could be stolen, and the attack is the hacker copying the files. The same pattern fits systems, applications, infrastructure, and people, for example an untrained employee (vulnerability) who falls for a phishing message sent by a social engineer (threat).

Attack	Actual attempt to exploit vulnerability	Active/deliberate exploitation	Hacker uses exploit to break into system

Topic 3
Threat Actors
By the end of this topic, the learner should be able to:

Define a threat actor and explain why identifying the attacker matters.
Describe the motivation and skill level of five types of threat actor.
Explain the difference between an external attacker and an insider threat.
The previous topic defined a threat as a potential source of harm. Behind every threat, however, there is a person or a group that creates it. These people and groups are called threat actors.(1)

A threat is the potential danger, while a threat actor is the person or group responsible for creating or carrying out that threat.

Identifying the attacker is important because each type has a different motivation and a different level of skill, and understanding an adversary is the first step toward defending against it. This topic introduces five types of threat actor: cybercriminals, nation-state actors, hacktivists, insider threats, and script kiddies.(1)

Cybercriminals are motivated above all by money. They attack in order to steal funds, to steal data that can be sold, or to lock systems and demand a payment to release them.(1)

This group is the most widespread type of threat actor, because its motivation is simple and direct, namely financial gain. A typical example is a group that sends fraudulent emails to thousands of people in order to steal their payment card details. This category seeks profit, but the next type is far more dangerous and is not driven by money.(1)


Nation-state actors are attackers supported by governments, and their motivation is political or military rather than financial. Their activities include spying on other countries and attempting to attack important infrastructure such as power stations.(1)

This is the most dangerous and most capable type of threat actor, because it is backed by enormous resources, funding, and highly skilled experts. Fortunately, these attacks usually target nations and governments rather than ordinary individuals. They are nonetheless important to recognise, because they are the reason cybersecurity has become a part of national security. While these groups are driven by politics, the next type is driven by a cause or a message.(1)


The word hacktivist combines hacker and activist, describing a hacker who acts in support of a cause. Hacktivists are not motivated by money, but by the wish to deliver a message or to defend a cause in which they believe.(1)

Their activities include altering the appearance of a website in order to display their message, or leaking information in order to expose something they regard as wrong. They use intrusion as a form of protest. It is important to note that this activity remains illegal, even when those who carry it out believe they are defending a just cause. This type comes from outside an organization, but danger can also arise from within.(1)


An insider threat is a danger that originates from a person inside the organization, such as an employee or anyone holding access permissions. This type is divided into two forms.(2)

Remember
Insider threats can be intentional or accidental. Both can cause serious security incidents.

The first form is deliberate: an employee who is dissatisfied or greedy leaks data or harms the organization on purpose. The second form is accidental: a well-meaning but careless employee opens a fraudulent email or loses a laptop, causing a problem without intending to. Insider threats are especially dangerous because the person is already inside and already holds permissions. For this reason, employee awareness reduces this risk significantly. The final type returns to outsiders, and it is the least skilled but the most numerous.(2)


Script kiddies are inexperienced attackers whose skills are limited. They do not know how to create attack tools of their own, but instead use ready-made tools created by others, often without fully understanding them.(1)


Their motivation is usually curiosity, or the wish to prove to themselves or to their friends that they are capable. They are the least dangerous in terms of skill, but the most numerous in terms of number, and they can still cause harm if they find a weak system that has been carelessly protected. Threat Actor	Main Motivation	Note
Cybercriminals	Money	The most widespread type
Nation-State Actors	Politics or military aims	The most dangerous and capable
Hacktivists	A cause or a message	Use intrusion as protest
Insider Threats	Varies; deliberate or accidental	Danger from inside the organization
Script Kiddies	Curiosity or proving themselves	Use ready-made tools
This topic introduced the main types of threat actor and their motivations.(1, 2)

Cybercriminals are motivated by money and are the most widespread.
Nation-state actors are politically motivated and the most capable.
Hacktivists are motivated by a cause, and insider threats come from within an organization.
Script kiddies are inexperienced attackers who rely on ready-made tools.

Topic 4
Attack Lifecycle
By the end of this topic, the learner should be able to:

Explain why defenders study the stages through which an attack progresses.
Describe the six stages of the attack lifecycle in order.
Explain why detecting an attack at an early stage reduces the damage.
The previous topics identified the attackers and the dangers they pose. This topic examines how an attack unfolds, because an attack does not happen in a single moment but progresses through a series of stages. These stages together are known as the attack lifecycle.(2)

Defenders study these stages for a clear reason. If the path an attack follows is understood, the attack can be stopped at any stage before it is completed, and the earlier it is detected, the less damage it causes. To make the stages easy to follow, each is compared to the plan of a burglar intending to rob a large building. There are six stages: reconnaissance, initial access, execution, persistence, lateral movement, and exfiltration.(3)

In the first stage, reconnaissance, the attacker gathers information about the target before attacking. In the burglar example, this is the stage of watching the building, learning when the guards leave and where the doors are located.(2)

In the digital domain, the attacker searches for information about an organization, its employees, and its systems, in order to find a weakness from which to begin. This connects directly to the idea of the digital footprint introduced in the first week: the more information that is publicly available about a target, the easier the reconnaissance stage becomes for the attacker. After gathering sufficient information, the attacker attempts to enter.(1)


In the second stage, initial access, the attacker takes the first step inside the system by finding a way in. In the burglar example, this is finding the open door or window and entering through it.(2)

In the digital domain, initial access is often achieved by deceiving an employee into opening a fraudulent email, or by exploiting a weak password. This is an important moment, because it is the first time the attacker is inside, and if the intrusion is detected here, the attacker can be removed quickly. Once inside, the attacker begins to carry out the purpose of the intrusion.(1)


In the third stage, execution, the attacker begins to run malicious programs or to carry out actions within the system. In the burglar example, this is the stage of actually searching the building for valuables.(2)

In the digital domain, the attacker runs harmful software, known as malware, in order to take control of a device or to begin gathering data. The types of malware used at this stage are examined in a later topic. A careful attacker, however, wishes to ensure continued access, which is the aim of the next stage.(2)


In the fourth stage, persistence, the attacker creates a means of returning, even if the original point of entry is closed. In the burglar example, this is making a copy of a key so that the building can be entered again at any time.(2)

In the digital domain, the attacker leaves a hidden back door, allowing a return to the system even after passwords are changed. For this reason, when a defender removes an attacker, it is essential to confirm that no means of return has been left behind. At this point the attacker is present on one device but wishes to reach more, which is the aim of the next stage.(2)


In the fifth stage, lateral movement, the attacker moves from the device that was entered to other devices and systems within the same network. In the burglar example, this is moving from room to room in search of the most valuable items.(2)

In the digital domain, the attacker travels from an ordinary employee’s device to more important devices that hold more valuable data, such as the device of a manager or the main server. The more the attacker moves, the greater the danger becomes, which is why monitoring the network helps to detect this unusual movement. After reaching the most valuable data, the attacker pursues the final goal.(2)


In the sixth and final stage, exfiltration, the attacker takes the data that has been reached and transfers it out to themselves. In the burglar example, this is carrying the stolen valuables out of the building.(2)


In the digital domain, the attacker copies sensitive data and sends it to their own device, in order to steal it, sell it, or use it to extort the organization. This is the stage at which the real harm, the impact, is realised. The table below summarises the six stages.

Persistence	Secures a way to return	Copying a key
Lateral Movement	Moves to other systems	Going room to room
Exfiltration	Takes the data out	Carrying the goods away
This topic described the six stages through which an attack progresses.(2, 3)

Reconnaissance gathers information, and initial access gains the first entry.
Execution runs malicious actions, and persistence secures a way to return.
Lateral movement spreads to other systems, and exfiltration removes the data.
Detecting an attack at any early stage reduces the damage it can cause.

Topic 5
Social Engineering Attacks
By the end of this topic, the learner should be able to:

Define social engineering and explain why it targets people rather than machines.
Describe five forms of social engineering and recognise the signs of each.
State the practical rules that protect against these attacks.
The previous topic observed that most attacks begin by deceiving a person rather than by breaking a machine. This method of attack is called social engineering, and it works by manipulating a person’s emotions and trust so that the victim hands over information or grants access willingly.(2)

This subject is especially important for everyday users, because it targets anyone who uses the internet. The purpose of this topic is therefore to enable the reader to recognise these attacks and to protect against them. Five forms are examined: phishing, spear phishing, vishing, smishing, and pretexting.(1)

Phishing is a fraudulent message, usually an email, that claims to come from a trusted source such as a bank or a well-known company. Its purpose is to deceive the recipient into entering their information or clicking on a harmful link.(2)

A typical phishing message warns that an account is in danger and urges the reader to click a link immediately and sign in to secure it. The link leads to a counterfeit website that resembles the genuine one, and any password entered there is sent to the attacker. Several warning signs help to identify phishing: a sense of urgency and fear, spelling mistakes within the message, an unfamiliar sender address, and links whose appearance is not quite correct.(2)

The guiding rule is never to click links in messages that cannot be verified, and never to enter a password except on the official website that the user has opened personally. Ordinary phishing is sent to thousands of people at once, but a more dangerous form is directed at a single person.(1)


Spear phishing is phishing aimed at one specific person rather than at the general public. The attacker first gathers information about the target, such as their name, school, and interests, and then sends a tailored message that appears highly convincing.(2)

Instead of a generic message, the victim receives a message addressed to them by name that refers to something they genuinely care about, making it far easier to trust. The distinction can be expressed as a comparison: ordinary phishing is like a net cast over a whole crowd, whereas spear phishing is like a spear aimed at one individual. This focus makes it more dangerous and harder to detect. The next forms move beyond written messages.(2)


The term vishing combines voice and phishing, and it describes deception carried out through a telephone call. The attacker calls the victim, claims to be from a bank or a technical support service, and attempts to persuade the victim to reveal sensitive information over the phone.(2)

A common example is a caller who claims to be from the bank, reports a problem with the account, and asks the victim to confirm the card number and password. The essential rule of protection is that a genuine organization will never ask for a password or a secret code over the telephone. If such a request is received, the correct response is to end the call and contact the official organization independently. Deception can also arrive through short text messages.(1)


The term smishing combines SMS, meaning a short text message, with phishing. In this form, the attacker sends a text message to the victim’s phone containing a harmful link or a request for information.(2)

A frequent example is a message announcing that the recipient has won a prize and inviting them to click a link to claim it, where the link is of course a trap. The same rule applies as before: a message that promises something unusually generous, or that frightens the recipient into acting quickly, is a sign of danger, and the link should not be clicked. The previous forms rely on a single message, but the final form relies on an entire invented story.(1)


The word pretext means an invented excuse or reason. Pretexting therefore describes an attack in which the attacker invents a false story or identity in order to win the victim’s trust and extract information.(2)


Rather than sending a single message, the attacker constructs a complete and convincing scenario. For example, a caller may claim to be from the information technology department of the victim’s school, offering to help with an account problem but requesting the password in order to fix it. The entire story is fabricated in order to earn trust. The protection is to verify a person’s identity through an official source before providing any information, however convincing the story may seem. The table below summarises the five forms.

Form	Method	Key Feature
Phishing	Fraudulent message to many people	Broad and general
Spear Phishing	Message aimed at one person	Tailored and convincing
Vishing	Deception by phone call	Uses the voice
Smishing	Deception by text message	Uses an SMS link
Pretexting	A complete invented story	Builds false trust
This topic examined social engineering, the deception of people in order to gain access or information.(1, 2)

Phishing is a fraudulent message sent broadly, while spear phishing targets one specific person.
Vishing uses a phone call, and smishing uses a text message.
Pretexting builds a complete false story to win trust.
All of these forms target emotions, so verifying before acting is the strongest protection.
Having examined attacks that target people, the next topic turns to attacks that target the devices themselves, in the form of malware.(3)

Topic 6
Malware Attacks
By the end of this topic, the learner should be able to:

Define malware and explain what the term means.
Describe how a virus, a worm, a Trojan, spyware, and ransomware behave.
State the common measures that protect against malware.
The previous topic examined how attackers deceive people. This topic turns to the weapon that attackers use against the devices themselves, which is known as malware. The word malware combines malicious, meaning harmful, with software, meaning a program, and it describes any harmful program created to damage a device or steal its data.(2)

Understanding the different forms of malware allows a user to recognise the danger and to protect against it. This topic examines five forms: the virus, the worm, the Trojan, spyware, and ransomware.(2)

A virus is a harmful program that attaches itself to a legitimate file or program and spreads when that file is run or passed from one person to another. It requires two things in order to spread: a file to attach to, and the action of a user opening that file.(2)

A virus can be compared to a common cold that passes between people when they come into contact, because some form of contact is required for it to spread. A typical example is a file downloaded from an untrusted source; the moment it is opened, the virus runs and begins to harm the device and to attempt to spread to other files. A virus depends on a user opening a file, but the next form spreads without waiting for anyone.(2)


A worm resembles a virus, but with one major difference: it spreads by itself, without needing a file to attach to and without requiring any action from a user. Once it enters a network, it copies itself and crawls from one device to another at great speed.(2)

A worm can be compared to an illness that spreads through the air without the need for contact, which makes it faster and more dangerous in its spread than a virus. A worm that enters a company network may reach hundreds of devices within minutes, without anyone clicking on anything. The previous forms reveal themselves as harmful, but the next form disguises itself as something useful.(2)


A Trojan is a harmful program that disguises itself as a legitimate and useful program, so that the user installs it while believing it to be safe. The name comes from the ancient story of the wooden horse of Troy, which appeared to be a gift but concealed soldiers inside.(2)

A Trojan therefore looks attractive on the outside but is dangerous within. It may appear to be a free game or a counterfeit program, but once installed it opens a door in the device for the attacker. The protection against this form is to download programs and games only from official sources, and never to install anything from unknown websites that promise paid software at no cost. The previous forms cause damage or spread, but the next form watches in silence.(1)


The word spyware derives from spy, and it describes a program that secretly observes the user and collects information without their knowledge. It may record what is typed on the keyboard, capture images of the screen, or track the websites that are visited, and then send all of this to the attacker.(2)

The danger of spyware lies in its silence, because a device may continue to operate normally while someone is observing it without the user’s awareness. Certain signs may suggest its presence, such as a device that suddenly becomes slow or an unusual amount of internet activity. Protection comes from using antivirus software and avoiding suspicious programs. The final form is widespread and openly demands payment.(2)


The word ransom refers to a payment demanded for release. Ransomware is a program that locks the user’s files or entire device and demands a payment in order to unlock them. It encrypts the data so that it remains present but cannot be opened, and then displays a message demanding payment for its return.(2)


This is one of the most dangerous forms, and it is responsible for halting entire companies and hospitals, as noted in the discussion of real-world incidents in the first week. The most important protection against it is simple: keeping a backup copy of one’s files in a separate location, so that even if the files are locked, another copy remains available. The table below summarises the five forms of malware.

Malware Type	Behaviour	Key Feature
Virus	Attaches to a file and spreads when opened	Needs a user to act
Worm	Spreads by itself across a network	Needs no user action
Trojan	Disguises itself as a safe program	Hidden danger
Spyware	Secretly watches and collects information	Operates in silence
Ransomware	Locks files and demands payment	Demands a ransom
This topic examined the main forms of malware and how to defend against them.(1, 2)

A virus attaches to a file and spreads when the file is opened.
A worm spreads by itself, and a Trojan disguises itself as a safe program.
Spyware watches in secret, and ransomware locks files and demands payment.
Common protections include trusted sources, antivirus software, updates, and backups.
Having examined attacks on people and on devices, the final topic of the week considers attacks that target the network itself.(2)

Topic 7
Network-Based Attacks
By the end of this topic, the learner should be able to:

Explain what a network-based attack is and why the network itself can be a target.
Describe a denial-of-service attack, a man-in-the-middle attack, a DNS attack, and packet sniffing.
Connect each attack to the principle of the CIA Triad that it threatens.
State the common measures that protect against these attacks.
The earlier topics examined attacks on people, through social engineering, and attacks on devices, through malware. This topic addresses the third category: attacks on the network itself, known as network-based attacks.(2)

The first week explained that devices are linked together over the internet in a connected system. The connections themselves can become a target for attack. This topic examines four forms: the denial-of-service attack, the man-in-the-middle attack, the DNS attack, and packet sniffing, with attention throughout to which principle of the CIA Triad each one threatens.(2)

A denial-of-service attack, in its larger form abbreviated as DDoS for distributed denial of service, floods a website or server with an enormous volume of false requests at the same moment, until the service can no longer cope and goes offline.(2)

The effect can be compared to a small shop into which a thousand false visitors suddenly crowd all at once, so that genuine customers can no longer enter or be served. This attack strikes directly at the principle of availability from the CIA Triad, because it prevents a service from remaining available to its users. The next attack threatens privacy by listening in on a connection.(2)


In a man-in-the-middle attack, the attacker secretly places themselves between the user and the website the user is communicating with, without either side noticing. All the information passing between them travels through the attacker first, who is able to read it and even to alter it.(2)

The situation can be compared to sending a letter to a friend through a messenger who opens and reads the letter, and who may change its contents, before delivering it, while neither the sender nor the recipient is aware. This attack threatens two principles of the CIA Triad: confidentiality, because the attacker reads the messages, and integrity, because the attacker can change them. Protection comes from noticing that secure websites begin their address with HTTPS, where the letter S indicates that the connection is encrypted, and from avoiding sensitive activities on public Wi-Fi networks. The next attack tampers with the directory of the internet itself.(2)


To understand this attack, the term DNS must first be explained. DNS stands for Domain Name System, and it can be compared to the telephone directory of the internet. When a user types the name of a website, the DNS translates that name into the real address of the site so that the device can reach it.(2)

In a DNS attack, the attacker tampers with this directory, so that when the user types the genuine name of a website, the altered directory sends them to a counterfeit site that closely resembles it. The user believes they are on the correct site, but are in fact on a trap, and any information they enter is sent to the attacker. Protection comes from always verifying the address of a website and looking for the secure HTTPS indicator before entering any sensitive information. The final attack relies on capturing data as it travels.(2)


Information travels across a network in small pieces called packets. The word sniffing means capturing, so packet sniffing describes an attacker capturing these packets as they move through the network and reading them.(2)

If the data is not encrypted, the attacker can see everything that is sent and received, including messages and passwords. The danger increases on public networks, such as the Wi-Fi found in cafes and public places, because anyone on the network may attempt to capture the data. The principal protection is encryption: when data is encrypted, an attacker who captures it cannot read it, finding only meaningless characters. The table below summarises the four attacks and the principle each one threatens.


Attack	What It Does	CIA Principle Threatened
Denial of Service	Floods a service until it goes offline	Availability
Man-in-the-Middle	Intercepts and alters communication	Confidentiality and Integrity
DNS Attack	Redirects users to a fake website	Confidentiality and Integrity
Packet Sniffing	Captures data travelling on the network	Confidentiality
This topic examined the main attacks that target the network itself.(1, 2)

A denial-of-service attack floods a service and threatens availability.
A man-in-the-middle attack intercepts communication and threatens confidentiality and integrity.
A DNS attack redirects users to a fake website, and packet sniffing captures data on the network.
Common protections include the secure HTTPS connection, encryption, and avoiding public networks for sensitive tasks.
This topic completes the study of cyber threats and attacks. The following week turns to the other side of the subject, examining how organizations defend themselves against all of these dangers.(2)

Topic 1
Introduction to Security Controls
By the end of this topic, the learner should be able to:

Define a security control and give everyday examples of one.
Explain the purpose of controls in protecting assets.
Explain how controls achieve risk reduction rather than complete safety.
Describe security management and the principle of defence in depth.
The first week of this course established the foundations of cybersecurity, and the second week examined the threats and attacks that endanger digital assets. This week turns to the most practical part of the subject: how organizations defend themselves against those attacks. (2)

The primary tool of defence is the security control. This topic introduces the idea by examining three matters: the purpose of controls, the way they reduce risk, and the organised process by which they are chosen and managed. These ideas prepare the reader for the specific types of control studied in the topics that follow. (4)

A security control is any measure or means put in place to protect assets and to reduce risk. In simple terms, it is any step taken to make the task of an attacker more difficult. (4)

Security controls help maintain the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information by preventing unauthorized access, detecting harmful activity, and supporting recovery when incidents occur.

Controls are not necessarily complex. The password on a phone is a control, the lock on the door of a house is a control, and even the habit of not opening unfamiliar links is a kind of control. A control is therefore any means of protection, whether it takes the form of a rule, a program, or a physical lock. (1)

Having defined what a control is, the next question concerns the reason for putting controls in place.


The central purpose of any control is to protect assets from the threats studied in the previous week. The assets identified earlier, namely data, devices, software, networks, and people, are precisely what controls exist to defend. (4)

In the same way, the threats studied earlier, such as malware, phishing, and network attacks, are precisely what controls exist to stop. A control therefore acts as the link that stands between a valuable asset and the danger that seeks to reach it. The most important outcome that controls achieve is the reduction of risk, which is examined next. (4)


The previous week defined risk as the likelihood that a threat exploits a vulnerability and causes harm, and explained that risk falls when vulnerabilities are closed. A security control is the tool that closes those vulnerabilities and thereby reduces risk. (4)

For example, a weak password is a vulnerability that increases risk; replacing it with a strong password and multi-factor authentication greatly reduces the chance that an attacker can exploit that weakness. An important truth must be stated clearly, however: there is no such thing as perfect safety. The goal is not to eliminate risk entirely, which is impossible, but to reduce it to the lowest level that can reasonably be achieved. This is the meaning of risk reduction. (4)


To select and apply the right controls, an organised approach is required, which is the final idea in this topic.

Some controls prevent attacks, others detect suspicious activity, and others help restore systems after an incident. Effective security uses all three.

Security management is the organised process by which suitable controls are chosen, applied, monitored, and updated. Controls are not placed at random; instead, an organization considers which assets are most important and which risks are greatest, and then applies the controls that best address each risk. (5)


A central principle of security management is known as defence in depth, which means layered protection. The idea is not to rely on a single means of protection, but to place many layers one behind another, so that if one layer fails, the layer behind it stops the attacker. (3)


An old castle illustrates the principle well. It is surrounded by a moat, a high wall, guards, and an iron gate. If an attacker passes one obstacle, others still remain. This layered arrangement is the essence of defence in depth, and it guides the way controls are combined. (3)

This topic introduced the security control as the primary tool of defence. (1, 4)

A security control is any measure put in place to protect assets and reduce risk.
The purpose of controls is to protect assets from threats.
Controls work by reducing risk, not by eliminating it completely.
Security management organises this work, guided by the principle of defence in depth.
Controls fall into three broad types. The next topic examines the first of these, which concerns people and rules, known as administrative controls. (5)

Topic 2
Administrative Controls
By the end of this topic, the learner should be able to:

Define administrative controls and explain why they focus on people and rules.
Distinguish between a policy, a procedure, and a standard.
Explain the role of security awareness training in protecting an organization.
The previous topic explained that security controls fall into three broad types. This topic examines the first type, the administrative controls, which are concerned not with programs or devices but with people and rules. They are the laws and the steps that organise the behaviour of employees so that they work safely. (5)

The first week described people as one of the pillars of protection. Administrative controls are the measures that strengthen that pillar. This topic examines four of them: policies, procedures, security awareness training, and standards. (2)

In addition to these, administrative controls also include governance and compliance activities, such as assigning security responsibilities, overseeing security decisions, and verifying that the organization follows its own rules and any applicable laws and regulations.

A policy is a general rule that states what is required and what is not permitted within an organization. It expresses what must happen, without entering into the detail of how it is to be done. (5)

For example, a policy may state that all employees must use strong passwords. This is a general rule that sets the goal. Policies can be compared to the general rules of a school, such as the requirement to arrive on time and to respect one’s classmates: they are principles that govern the conduct of everyone. A policy states what is required, but it does not state the exact steps, which is the role of the next control. (5)


A procedure is the detailed set of steps that explains exactly how a policy is to be carried out, one step at a time. Where a policy sets the goal, a procedure provides the method. (5)

For instance, if a policy requires employees to report any suspicious email, the procedure explains how: first, do not click any link; second, send the email to the security department; third, delete it. The distinction is therefore simple and clear: a policy states what is required, while a procedure states how it is to be done. The value of procedures is that they cause everyone to act in the same correct manner, which reduces mistakes. Rules and steps are of little use, however, if people do not understand them or are unaware of the danger, which is the role of the next control. (5)


Security awareness training is instruction given to employees so that they learn to recognise threats and respond correctly. It directly addresses the human weakness that attackers exploit. (2)

The previous week examined social engineering attacks such as phishing, and the strongest defence against them is for people to be aware and able to recognise them. This training teaches an employee how to distinguish a genuine email from a fraudulent one, how to create a strong password, and what to do when something appears suspicious. Because people are often the weakest point, raising their awareness closes the largest vulnerability of all. Awareness training is most effective when it is continuous rather than a single event: regular refresher sessions, simulated phishing exercises, and updated guidance keep employees prepared as threats evolve. The final control returns precision to the rules by expressing them in exact terms. (2)


A standard is a specific and mandatory requirement that converts a general policy into a precise condition that everyone must follow. It removes the vagueness that a policy alone may leave. (5)


Returning to the earlier example, a policy may require a strong password, but the word strong is open to interpretation. A standard removes the ambiguity by stating exactly what is required, for instance that a password must be no fewer than twelve characters and must contain numbers and symbols. In this way, a policy sets the goal, a standard sets the precise condition, and a procedure sets the steps, so that the three work together. For example, a company policy requires strong passwords. The standard specifies that passwords must contain at least 12 characters, numbers, and symbols. The procedure explains how employees create or reset their passwords in the company system. The table below summarises the four administrative controls.

Control	What It Does	Example
Policy	States the general requirement	Passwords must be strong
Procedure	Gives the detailed steps	How to report a suspicious email
Awareness Training	Teaches people to recognise threats	Spotting a phishing message
Standard	Sets a precise, mandatory condition	At least twelve characters
This topic examined the administrative controls, which protect through people and rules. (5, 2)

Policies are general rules that state what is required.
Procedures are the detailed steps that explain how to carry out a policy.
Security awareness training teaches people to recognise threats.
Standards are precise, mandatory conditions that remove ambiguity.
Administrative controls form the first layer of defense by guiding employee behavior and reducing human errors before technical or physical controls are needed.

Administrative controls protect by organising the behaviour of people, but people alone are not enough. The next topic examines the technological tools that protect alongside them, known as technical controls. (3)

Topic 3
Technical Controls
By the end of this topic, the learner should be able to:

Define technical controls and explain their role in defence.
Describe the function of a firewall, antivirus, EDR, MFA, and a SIEM.
Connect each technical control to the threat it helps to defend against.
The previous topic examined the controls that organise the behaviour of people. This topic examines the second type, the technical controls, which are the tools and programs that protect systems by technological means. They are the technology that stands directly in the path of an attacker. (3)

Each of the tools examined here defends against a particular kind of threat studied in the previous week. This topic introduces five of them: the firewall, antivirus, EDR, MFA, and the SIEM. (3)

A firewall is a guard that controls the connections entering and leaving a network, and decides which are permitted to pass and which are blocked. It can be imagined as a checkpoint at a gate. (3)

Every connection that seeks to enter the network must first pass through the firewall. If the connection is trusted, it is allowed through; if it is suspicious, it is blocked. In this way, the firewall forms the first line of defence at the boundary of the network, stopping many harmful connections before they ever reach the devices inside. A firewall protects the boundary, but if something harmful passes through, the device itself must be protected, which is the role of the next tool. (3)


Antivirus is a program that detects the malware studied in the previous week and removes it from a device. The viruses, Trojans, and spyware examined earlier are precisely what it is designed to find. (3)

Antivirus operates much like the immune system of the body: it scans files, and as soon as it finds something harmful, it acts to remove it. To remain effective, it must be updated continually, so that it can recognise the new forms of malware that appear each day. Antivirus is valuable, but attackers have grown more sophisticated, which led to a newer and more capable tool. (3)


The term EDR stands for Endpoint Detection and Response. An endpoint is any device at the edge of a network, such as a laptop or a phone, and EDR is an advanced protective system installed on such devices. (3)

The difference between EDR and antivirus is significant. Antivirus focuses on finding and removing malware, and while modern antivirus solutions often include behavioural and heuristic detection in addition to recognising known malware, their role remains centred on detection and removal. EDR goes further by continuously monitoring the behaviour of the whole device, so that if it notices unusual activity, even from a new and unknown program, it detects the activity and acts at once. It does not merely detect but also provides investigation and response capabilities, for example by isolating an infected device to prevent the problem from spreading. The tools so far have protected networks and devices; the next tool protects the accounts that attackers most often target. (3)


The term MFA stands for Multi-Factor Authentication. The first week introduced authentication as the process that confirms a user’s identity, and MFA strengthens that process. (3)

Instead of relying on a password alone, MFA requires a second proof of identity. After the password is entered, the system sends a code to the user’s phone or requests a fingerprint. This is highly effective, because even if an attacker discovers the password, they cannot enter without the phone or the fingerprint, and so they are stopped at the second barrier. MFA is among the simplest and strongest protections available, and it is recommended for all accounts. All of these tools generate many events, and a final tool gathers and analyses them. (3)


The term SIEM stands for Security Information and Event Management. A SIEM is a system that collects the records and events from all the devices and tools across an organization into one place and analyses them together. (2)


This is valuable because a single event seen alone may appear ordinary, but when a SIEM gathers many events from different places, it can link them and detect signs of an attack. It can be compared to a control room that holds all of the monitoring screens in one place, where the person watching sees the complete picture and notices what could not be seen on any single screen. A SIEM therefore does not replace human analysts; it supports them by gathering and correlating events so that the security team can investigate and respond more effectively. The SIEM is the heart of the security operations centre examined in the final topic of the week. The table below summarises the five technical controls.

Control	Function	Defends Against
Firewall	Controls connections at the network boundary	Harmful network connections
Antivirus	Detects and removes malware	Viruses, Trojans, spyware
EDR	Watches device behaviour and responds	New and unknown threats
MFA	Adds a second proof of identity	Stolen passwords
SIEM	Collects and analyses events centrally	Hidden, coordinated attacks
This topic examined the technical controls that protect systems by technological means. (3, 2)

A firewall controls connections at the boundary of the network.
Antivirus detects and removes malware, and EDR watches device behaviour and responds.
MFA protects accounts with a second proof of identity beyond the password.
A SIEM collects and analyses events from across the organization to detect attacks.
For example, a phishing email bypasses the firewall, antivirus detects the malicious attachment, EDR isolates the infected device, MFA prevents the attacker from accessing user accounts, and the SIEM alerts the security team by combining events from multiple systems.

Technical controls provide automated protection, but they are most effective when combined with administrative controls, such as security policies and awareness training, and physical controls that protect facilities and equipment.

Rules and technology have now been covered, but one type of control remains, concerning the protection of physical objects that can be touched. This is the subject of the next topic. (5)

Topic 4
Physical Controls
By the end of this topic, the learner should be able to:

Explain why cybersecurity includes the protection of physical objects.
Describe the role of security guards, CCTV, locks, and access badges.
Connect physical controls to the principles of authorization and accountability.
The previous topics examined the protection of rules and people, and protection through technology. This topic examines the third and final type, the physical controls, which protect the objects that can be touched.(3)

An important point is often overlooked: cybersecurity is not only a matter of programs. If an attacker steals the server itself, or enters the room in which the devices are kept, they reach the data without needing to break any program. For this reason, physical objects must be protected. This topic examines four measures: security guards, CCTV, locks, and access badges.(3)

Security guards are people who stand at the entrances and confirm that those who enter are permitted to do so. They form the human line of defence at the door.(3)

Guards watch, question, and prevent the entry of any stranger or suspicious person. Their particular strength is that they can make a judgement in the moment, which neither a camera nor a lock can do; if something unusual occurs, a guard reacts immediately. A guard, however, cannot watch everywhere at once, and so additional eyes are needed to observe continuously, which is the role of the next control.(3)


The term CCTV stands for Closed-Circuit Television, and it refers simply to the cameras that record what takes place. These cameras operate in two ways.(3)

The first is deterrence: the moment a person sees a camera, they think carefully before doing anything wrong. The second is the recording of evidence: if a problem occurs, the recording can be examined to determine who did what and when. The first week introduced the principle of accountability, which holds that every action should be recorded. CCTV is accountability applied to the physical world. Guards and cameras observe, but a means is also needed to close doors physically, which is the role of the next control.(3)


Locks are the devices that secure doors and sensitive rooms, such as the room that holds the servers. They are the simplest and oldest measure, yet they remain among the most important, because they directly prevent physical access to the most valuable devices.(3)

Locks may be traditional, opened with a key, or electronic, opened with a code or a fingerprint, but the purpose is the same: a door should open only for a person who has permission. In the digital world, protection is provided by a password, and in the physical world, it is provided by a lock; the idea is the same, applied in two different places. In a large building with many people and many doors, however, ordinary locks become impractical, and a more intelligent system is required.(3)


An access badge is a card carried by an employee that determines exactly where they are permitted to enter. The card is held near a reader at the door, and the door opens only if the holder is authorised to enter that particular place.(3)


The first week introduced authorization, which determines what a user is permitted to do. The access badge is authorization applied to the physical world. An ordinary employee’s card opens the ordinary doors, but the server room opens only for the cards of those who are specifically permitted. A further benefit is that the card records who entered which place and when, providing accountability as well. The table below summarises the four physical controls.

Control	What It Does	Related Principle
Security Guards	Human judgement at the entrance	Prevention and response
CCTV	Records activity and deters wrongdoing	Accountability
Locks	Prevent physical access to devices	Confidentiality
Access Badges	Control who may enter which place	Authorization and accountability
This topic examined the physical controls that protect objects that can be touched.(1, 3)

Security guards provide human judgement at the entrance.
CCTV records activity and deters wrongdoing.
Locks prevent physical access to important devices.
Access badges control who may enter which place and record their entry.
The three types of control have now been examined. The next topic considers a different way of classifying controls, according to the function each one performs.(2)

Topic 5
Control Functions
By the end of this topic, the learner should be able to:

Explain that controls can be classified by their function as well as by their type.
Describe the five control functions and when each one acts relative to an attack.
Give an example of each function.
The previous topics classified controls by their type as administrative, technical, or physical. This topic introduces a second and equally important way of classifying them, according to their function, that is, according to what each control actually does and when it acts in relation to an attack. (4)

The first week described the goals of security as protecting, detecting, and responding, and this classification develops that idea further. Five functions are examined, arranged by their timing relative to an attack, before it, during it, and after it: preventive, detective, corrective, recovery, and compensating. (4)

A preventive control acts before an attack, in order to stop it from happening at all. Its aim is to halt a problem before it ever begins. (4)

Examples include the lock on a door, the firewall, multi-factor authentication, and the training of employees, all of which prevent an attack before it starts. This is the most desirable function, because prevention is always better than cure. It must be acknowledged, however, that no prevention is perfect, which is why the next function is needed. If an attack does succeed and begins, something must detect it. (4)


A detective control acts during an attack, or after it has occurred, by detecting that it is taking place. It does not prevent the attack, but it reveals it and gives warning that something is wrong. (4)

Examples include CCTV cameras, alerting systems, the records known as logs, and the SIEM. The previous week examined the attack lifecycle, and detective controls are what allow defenders to catch an attacker at any stage before the attack is completed. Once an attack has been detected, the damage must be stopped, which is the role of the next function. (4)


A corrective control acts after an attack has been detected, in order to stop the damage and repair the problem. Once it is known that an attack is occurring, the corrective control works to limit it. (4)

Examples include removing malware from a device, isolating an infected device from the network so that the problem cannot spread, and closing an account that has been compromised. Where the detective control announces that there is a problem, the corrective control stops and repairs it. The damage may be stopped, but the affected systems must then be restored, which is the role of the next function. (4)


A recovery control restores systems and data to their normal state after an attack. Its purpose is to return operations to working order and to make services available once again, which connects to the principle of availability from the first week. (4)

The best-known example is the backup, the spare copy of data. The previous week noted that a backup is the protection against ransomware, and a backup is precisely a recovery control. If files are locked or deleted, the recovery control restores them from the spare copy, as though the attack had not occurred. The distinction between corrective and recovery controls is worth stating clearly: a corrective control deals with the incident itself, by stopping the damage and fixing the immediate problem, whereas a recovery control deals with the aftermath, by returning systems, data, and services to their normal working state. In short, correction ends the harm, while recovery restores what was lost. One function remains, which is used when the ideal control cannot be applied. (4)


A compensating control is an alternative measure used when the ideal control cannot be applied for some reason. It is a substitute that makes up for the absence of the primary measure. (4)


An everyday comparison helps to make the idea clear. If the alarm in a shop has broken and has not yet been repaired, an additional guard may be placed to watch until it is fixed; the additional guard is a compensating control. In the digital world, if an older system cannot support multi-factor authentication, the organization may compensate by monitoring that system more closely and limiting who can reach it. This is not the ideal solution, but it reduces the risk until a better one is found. The table below summarises the five functions by their timing.

Function	Timing	Example
Preventive	Before the attack	Lock, firewall, MFA, training
Detective	During the attack	CCTV, alerts, logs, SIEM
Corrective	After detection	Removing malware, isolating a device
Recovery	After the attack	Restoring data from a backup
Compensating	When the ideal control is unavailable	Extra monitoring as a substitute
This topic classified controls by the function each one performs. (4, 1)

A preventive control acts before an attack to stop it.
A detective control acts during an attack to reveal it.
A corrective control stops the damage, and a recovery control restores normal operation.
A compensating control is a substitute used when the ideal control is unavailable.
The detective function proved especially important, and the next topic examines it in practice, under the heading of monitoring and detection. (4)

Topic 6
Monitoring and Detection
By the end of this topic, the learner should be able to:

Explain how detection works in practice through a sequence of steps.
Describe logging, alerting, security monitoring, and threat detection.
Explain why distinguishing a real threat from a false alarm is important.
The previous topic showed that the detective function is essential to defence. This topic examines that function in practice and explains how detection actually works, through four steps that build upon one another.(2)

The four steps are logging, alerting, security monitoring, and threat detection. They form a logical sequence that runs from the recording of an event to the identification of a genuine threat, and each step depends on the one before it.(2)

Logging is the recording of every event that occurs within a system into a record known as a log. The log states who entered, at what time, what was opened, and what was done.(2)

The first week introduced the principle of accountability, that every action should be recorded, and logging is the practical application of that principle. Logs are the foundation of everything that follows, because nothing can be detected without a record of it; they are the raw material on which detection works. Logs, however, are enormous, containing thousands of events every second, far too many for any person to read by hand, which is why a means is needed to draw attention to what matters.(2)


Alerting is the automatic notification that a system sends when it finds something suspicious within the logs. Instead of a person searching through millions of events, the system itself raises its hand and signals that something is unusual.(2)

For example, if an account attempts to log in incorrectly fifty times in one minute, this is not normal behaviour, and the system immediately produces an alert. Alerting can be compared to a fire alarm, which does not wait for someone to notice the smoke but sounds by itself the moment it senses danger. Alerts indicate where to look, but a person must actually be watching at all times, which is the next step.(2)


Security monitoring is the continuous observation of systems and alerts, carried out around the clock. A team or a system watches the screens at all times, every hour of every day.(1)

This continuous watch is necessary because attackers do not rest and may strike at any time, even in the middle of the night, so the watch must never stop. It can be compared to a guard in a control room whose eyes are on all of the cameras at once, waiting for any unusual movement. Not every alert, however, represents a genuine attack; some are false alarms, and so a final step is required to tell them apart.(1)


Threat detection is the analysis of alerts and data in order to separate a genuine threat from a false alarm. If every alert were treated as a disaster, time and effort would be wasted, yet if an important alert were ignored, a real attack might be missed.(2)


Threat detection is therefore the skill that judges which alert is merely an ordinary error and which is a genuine threat requiring immediate action. It can be compared to a doctor examining test results, who distinguishes what is normal from what requires urgent attention. The same judgement is applied here, but to the security of systems. The table below summarises the four steps as a sequence.

Step	What It Does	Comparison
Logging	Records every event	Keeping a written record
Alerting	Signals suspicious events automatically	A fire alarm
Security Monitoring	Watches continuously, around the clock	A guard in a control room
Threat Detection	Separates real threats from false alarms	A doctor reading test results
This topic explained how detection works through a sequence of four steps.(1, 2)

Logging records every event that occurs.
Alerting automatically signals suspicious events.
Security monitoring watches systems continuously, around the clock.
Threat detection separates genuine threats from false alarms.
These four steps do not operate on their own. They are carried out by a dedicated team in a dedicated place, the security operations centre, which is the subject of the final topic.(1)

Topic 7
Security Operations Center (SOC)
By the end of this topic, the learner should be able to:

Explain the purpose of a Security Operations Center.
Describe the steps of the SOC workflow.
Describe the main responsibilities of a security analyst.
The previous topic explained that monitoring and detection are carried out by a dedicated team in a dedicated place. That place is called the Security Operations Center, abbreviated as the SOC, and it brings together everything studied in this week into a working defence. (2)

This final topic of the course examines three matters: the purpose of a SOC, the way it works, and the responsibilities of the security analysts who staff it. It then draws together the whole of the course into a single picture. (2)

A SOC is a central team whose task is to monitor, detect, and respond to threats around the clock. It can be imagined as the central control room of a large system, or as a fire station whose staff remain ready at all times and move at once when danger appears. (2)

The first week described the goals of security as protecting, detecting, and responding, and the SOC is the place where these goals are carried out in practice. The previous topic introduced the SIEM, the system that gathers all events into one place, and the SIEM is the principal tool on which the SOC team works. Although the SIEM is a central platform in many SOCs, analysts also use tools such as Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR), threat intelligence platforms, vulnerability management tools, and case management systems to investigate and respond to security incidents. The way the team acts when an attack occurs follows a defined order, which is examined next. (4)


The SOC operates through an ordered sequence of steps, and that sequence draws together everything studied during the week. The first step is monitoring, in which the team continuously watches the screens and the logs. The second step is detection, in which an alert is raised about something suspicious and the team takes notice. The third step is investigation, in which the team examines the alert to determine whether it is a genuine threat or a false alarm. (2)


The fourth step is response, in which, if a genuine threat is confirmed, the team acts to stop the attack and limit its damage. The fifth step is recovery, in which, after the attack has been stopped, the systems are restored to their normal state and lessons are drawn to prevent the same attack from happening again. After recovery, security teams review the incident, identify lessons learned, update detection rules, and improve security controls to reduce the likelihood of similar attacks in the future. In this way, everything studied during the week operates together inside the SOC as a single, complete process. The table below summarises the workflow.

Step	Action
Monitoring	Continuously watch screens and logs
Detection	Notice an alert about something suspicious
Investigation	Decide whether the alert is a real threat
Response	Stop the attack and limit the damage
Recovery	Restore systems and learn from the event
The security analyst is the person who works within the SOC and forms the first line of defence. The role carries several responsibilities. (2)

The analyst continuously watches the screens and the alerts, sorts the alerts to decide which are important and which are false alarms, and investigates the important ones in order to understand what is happening. If a large attack is discovered, the analyst escalates it to a more specialised team, such as the incident response team, to deal with it. Security analysts also document incidents, communicate with other security teams, and help improve detection rules based on the incidents they investigate. The first week introduced the roles of the SOC analyst and the incident responder among the cybersecurity career tracks, and this topic shows how those roles operate together in practice. (2)


This topic examined the Security Operations Center, where defence is put into practice. (2, 4)

A SOC is a central team that monitors, detects, and responds to threats around the clock.
The SOC workflow proceeds through monitoring, detection, investigation, response, and recovery.
A security analyst watches, sorts, and investigates alerts, and escalates large attacks.
The SOC brings together the technical, human, and procedural elements of defence into one process.
This topic concludes the course. The first week established the foundations of cybersecurity and its career landscape, the second week examined the threats and attacks that endanger digital assets, and this third week explained how organizations defend themselves through controls, monitoring, and the work of the SOC. The learner now holds a complete picture of the field: what is protected, where the danger comes from, and how protection is achieved. (2)

Although the tools have changed, the fundamental principles of art remain the same. Artists still rely on composition, color, lighting, balance, and visual communication to create effective and meaningful designs. Digital tools simply provide new ways to express creativity and make the creative process faster and more flexible.

Digital art plays an important role in many industries. It helps businesses communicate ideas, entertain audiences, educate learners, and create interactive experiences.

Topic 2: Introduction to Basic Design
Art and design are closely related creative fields, but they serve different purposes. Art focuses on expressing ideas, emotions, and personal experiences, while design aims to solve problems and communicate messages effectively. Understanding the differences between art and design helps students recognize how creativity is applied in everyday life, from paintings and illustrations to logos, mobile applications, and advertisements.

This lesson introduces the fundamental concepts of art and design, explains how design functions as a form of visual communication, and explores the design process used to create effective solutions.

Art is a form of creative expression that allows artists to communicate their thoughts, emotions, experiences, and imagination. Unlike design, art does not always have a practical purpose or solve a specific problem. Instead, it encourages personal interpretation and emotional connection.

Lesson illustration
Artists use elements such as color, shape, texture, and composition to express ideas in unique ways. Different viewers may interpret the same artwork differently based on their own experiences and perspectives.

Because art is subjective, there is often no single "correct" meaning. Instead, its value comes from the ideas and emotions it communicates.

Design is the process of creating visual solutions that fulfill a specific purpose or solve a particular problem. Unlike art, design is created with a clear audience and objective in mind.

Lesson illustration
Design is used in many areas of everyday life, including logos, websites, mobile applications, packaging, posters, and product interfaces. An effective design should be clear, functional, visually appealing, and easy to understand.

Successful designs are carefully planned so that users can quickly recognize information, navigate products, or remember a brand.

Although art and design both involve creativity and visual skills, they have different goals.

Art	Design
Expresses ideas and emotions	Solves problems and communicates information
Personal and open to interpretation	Created for a specific audience and purpose
Focuses on creative expression	Focuses on usability and effectiveness
May have multiple meanings	Delivers a clear and intended message
Many modern creative projects combine both art and design. For example, a video game may include artistic illustrations while also using carefully designed user interfaces that help players interact with the game.

Visual communication is the process of conveying information through images, symbols, colors, typography, and other visual elements instead of relying only on written text.

Good design allows people to understand information quickly because many visual symbols have widely recognized meanings.

Some common examples include:

A check mark (✓) usually indicates a correct action, confirmation, or successful completion.
An X symbol (✕) often represents an error, cancellation, or closing a window.
A trash bin icon commonly represents deleting files or removing content.
A video camera icon typically indicates the option to start a video call.
These visual symbols improve usability by helping users understand actions without reading lengthy instructions.

Design is an iterative process, meaning designers improve their work through multiple stages rather than producing the final solution immediately.

A typical design process includes:

1. Understanding the Problem

Designers first identify the user's needs, project goals, and design requirements.

2. Generating Ideas

Initial ideas are explored through brainstorming and rough sketches. At this stage, designers experiment with multiple possible solutions.

3. Creating a Prototype

After selecting a promising idea, designers create a more detailed version using digital design software or other tools.

4. Collecting Feedback

Clients, users, or team members review the design and provide feedback. This helps identify areas that can be improved.

5. Refining the Design

Designers revise their work based on the feedback, making adjustments until the final solution effectively meets the project's goals.

This step-by-step process allows designers to create solutions that are functional, user-friendly, and visually effective.

Learning the difference between art and design helps students choose appropriate creative approaches for different situations.

Artists focus on expressing ideas and emotions, while designers focus on solving communication challenges. Understanding both disciplines provides a strong foundation for future studies in branding, illustration, animation, game development, user interface design, and many other creative fields.

Art and design both use creativity, but they serve different purposes. Art focuses on personal expression and emotional communication, while design is created to solve problems and communicate information clearly.

Design relies on visual communication through symbols, colors, typography, and layout, allowing users to understand information quickly. Designers also follow an organized process that includes planning, sketching, prototyping, gathering feedback, and refining their work to produce effective solutions.

Understanding these concepts provides the foundation for learning more advanced design skills throughout the Digital Arts course.

Topic 3: Elements of Art
Every artwork is built from a set of fundamental visual components known as the elements of art. These elements help artists organize ideas, create realistic objects, communicate emotions, and guide the viewer's attention. Whether creating a traditional drawing or a digital illustration, understanding these elements is essential for producing strong and effective visual compositions.

Lesson illustration
This lesson introduces the primary elements of art—line, shape, form, value, and texture—and explains how they work together to create engaging artwork.

The elements of art are the basic building blocks used to create visual compositions. Artists combine these elements in different ways to communicate ideas, create depth, establish mood, and guide the viewer's attention.

The primary elements of art include:

Line
Shape
Form
Value
Texture
Understanding these elements provides the foundation for creating successful artwork in both traditional and digital media.

A line is the path created when a point moves across a surface. Lines are one of the most fundamental elements of art because they define shapes, separate spaces, suggest movement, and guide the viewer's eye through a composition.

Lesson illustration
Lines can vary in appearance, including:

Straight
Curved
Horizontal
Vertical
Diagonal
Thick
Thin
Smooth
Zigzag
Different types of lines create different visual effects. For example, straight lines often suggest stability and order, while curved lines can create a sense of softness or movement.

A shape is a two-dimensional area enclosed by lines or edges. Shapes have height and width but do not have depth.

Lesson illustration
Common geometric shapes include:

Circle
Square
Rectangle
Triangle
Artists also use organic shapes, which have irregular outlines inspired by nature, such as leaves, clouds, or rocks.

Shapes serve as the foundation for more complex drawings and designs because many objects can be simplified into basic geometric forms.

A form is a three-dimensional object that has height, width, and depth. Forms are created by adding the illusion of volume to a shape or by representing actual three-dimensional objects.

Lesson illustration
Examples include:

A circle becomes a sphere.
A square becomes a cube.
A triangle can become a pyramid.
Artists create the illusion of form by using perspective, lighting, and shading, making objects appear more realistic.

Value refers to the lightness or darkness of a color or object. It is created by adding highlights and shadows to show how light interacts with surfaces.

Lesson illustration
Value is important because it helps artists:

Create depth
Show volume
Suggest lighting conditions
Make objects appear three-dimensional
Without changes in value, objects often appear flat. By carefully adjusting light and shadow, artists create realistic forms and improve the visual impact of their artwork.

Texture describes how a surface looks or feels. In visual art, texture may be either actual (physically felt) or implied (visually suggested).

Lesson illustration
Common textures include:

Smooth
Rough
Soft
Hard
Metallic
Wooden
Stone
Fabric
Artists use texture to make objects appear more realistic and to add visual interest. Even in digital art, texture helps viewers imagine how a surface would feel if they could touch it.

The elements of art rarely work independently. Instead, artists combine multiple elements to create balanced, engaging, and meaningful compositions.

For example:

Thick and thin lines create visual contrast.
Rough and smooth textures make objects more interesting.
Light and dark values create depth and focus.
Different shapes and forms organize objects within a scene.
When these elements are combined effectively, they improve the overall composition and help communicate the artist's intended message.

Using Value to Create Silhouettes

One-way artists use value is by creating a silhouette. A silhouette is the dark outline of an object seen against a brighter background, making the object's overall shape clearly recognizable while hiding its internal details.

Lesson illustration
Silhouettes are commonly seen during sunsets, where strong backlighting causes people, trees, or buildings to appear as solid dark shapes against the bright sky.

Designers and illustrators also use silhouettes because they create strong visual contrast and make subjects easy to identify.

The elements of art are the foundation of all visual creations. Lines define direction and movement, shapes create two-dimensional objects, forms add depth, values produce light and shadow, and textures create realistic surfaces.

These elements work together to build compositions that communicate ideas, create realism, and guide the viewer's attention. Mastering these fundamentals prepares students for more advanced topics in digital illustration, animation, graphic design, and game art.

Topic 4: Principles of Design and Gestalt Principles
Creating an effective design involves more than arranging shapes and colors on a page. Designers use a set of visual guidelines known as the principles of design to organize elements in a way that is attractive, balanced, and easy to understand. These principles help guide the viewer's attention, create harmony, and communicate messages clearly.

In addition, designers apply Gestalt principles, which explain how people naturally organize and interpret visual information. Understanding these psychological principles helps designers create layouts that are intuitive and visually engaging.

The principles of design are guidelines that help designers organize visual elements into clear and effective compositions. While the elements of art are the building blocks of a design, the principles of design explain how those elements should be arranged to create visual harmony and communicate ideas successfully.

Lesson illustration
Designers use these principles in graphic design, branding, advertising, illustration, animation, websites, and user interfaces.

Balance refers to the visual distribution of elements within a composition. A balanced design feels stable and comfortable because visual weight is distributed evenly across the layout.

There are different ways to achieve balance, including:

Lesson illustration
Symmetrical balance, where elements are evenly arranged on both sides of a central axis.
Lesson illustration
Asymmetrical balance, where different-sized elements create visual equilibrium without being identical.
Lesson illustration
Radial balance, where elements are arranged around a central point.
Balanced compositions help viewers focus on the content without feeling distracted or overwhelmed.

Contrast is created by placing different visual elements next to one another to make important information stand out.

Lesson illustration
Designers commonly create contrast using differences in:

Light and dark colors
Large and small sizes
Thick and thin lines
Different shapes
Bold and regular typography
Strong contrast improves readability, attracts attention, and highlights important information within a design.

Emphasis is the principle of making one element the main point of focus. It directs the viewer's attention to the most important part of the composition before they notice other elements.

Lesson illustration
Designers create emphasis by using:

Larger sizes
Bright or contrasting colors
Bold typography
Strategic placement
Extra spacing around important content
For example, promotional posters often emphasize discounts, product names, or key messages so viewers immediately notice them.

Movement refers to the visual path that guides the viewer's eyes through a design.

Lesson illustration
Designers create movement by arranging elements in ways that naturally lead the eye from one area to another. This can be achieved through:

Directional lines
Curved shapes
Repeated patterns
Gradual changes in size or color
The positioning of objects
Effective movement helps viewers understand information in the intended order.

Unity is the sense that all parts of a design belong together and contribute to one complete visual message.

Lesson illustration
Designers achieve unity by maintaining consistency in:

Color palettes
Typography
Shapes
Illustration styles
Spacing
Visual themes
When unity is present, a design appears organized, professional, and visually cohesive.

Proportion refers to the relationship between the sizes of different elements within a composition.

Lesson illustration
Proper proportions make objects appear realistic and visually balanced. However, designers sometimes intentionally exaggerate proportions to create a particular artistic style or communicate a specific idea.

For example:

Realistic illustrations maintain natural body proportions.
Cartoon characters often have oversized heads or exaggerated features to appear more expressive or friendly.
Choosing appropriate proportions depends on the purpose and style of the design.

The Gestalt Principles are a set of psychological concepts that explain how people naturally organize visual information into meaningful patterns. Rather than seeing individual shapes separately, the human brain automatically groups related elements together.

Designers use these principles to improve readability, organization, and user experience.

Proximity states that objects placed close to one another are perceived as belonging to the same group.

Lesson illustration
For example, buttons placed together in a navigation menu are naturally understood as related options.

Grouping related information using spacing helps users quickly understand layouts and reduces visual confusion.

Similarity explains that objects sharing similar characteristics—such as color, shape, size, or texture—are perceived as belonging together, even if they are separated by distance.

Designers often use similarity to organize information by giving related items the same visual appearance.

Examples include:

Navigation buttons with the same color and shape.
Icons that share a common design style.
Headings using the same font and size.
Continuation

Continuation refers to the tendency of the human eye to follow lines, curves, or patterns smoothly, even when parts of them are interrupted.

Lesson illustration
Instead of seeing separate segments, viewers naturally perceive one continuous path.

Designers use continuation to guide users through layouts and create a natural reading flow.

Closure is the tendency to mentally complete incomplete shapes or figures.

Even when parts of an object are missing, the brain fills in the gaps to recognize the complete image.

Many modern logos use closure by suggesting shapes without drawing every outline, creating simple yet memorable designs.

Figure–Ground describes how people distinguish the main subject (figure) from its background (ground).

Good visual design creates a clear distinction between important content and the surrounding space, allowing viewers to focus on the intended subject.

Some famous visual illusions use this principle by allowing viewers to interpret the same image in two different ways, depending on whether they perceive the figure or the background first.

Design principles and Gestalt principles work together to improve visual communication. They help designers organize information, highlight important content, create attractive layouts, and guide viewers naturally through a composition.

These concepts are widely applied in branding, advertising, user interface design, illustration, animation, product packaging, and digital media. Mastering them provides a strong foundation for creating clear, functional, and visually appealing designs.

The principles of design help organize visual elements into balanced and effective compositions. Balance, contrast, emphasis, movement, unity, and proportion each contribute to making designs attractive and easy to understand.

Gestalt principles explain how people naturally interpret visual information. Concepts such as proximity, similarity, continuation, closure, and figure–ground help designers organize content in ways that feel intuitive and improve user experience.

Together, these principles enable designers to communicate ideas clearly and create visually engaging work across a wide range of creative fields.

Topic 6: Composition and Visual Thinking
A successful design is not only about creating attractive visual elements but also about arranging them effectively. Composition is the process of organizing visual elements to guide the viewer's attention and communicate ideas clearly. Designers use composition techniques to create balance, emphasize important information, and make designs more engaging.

Visual thinking complements composition by encouraging designers to observe the world carefully, recognize visual patterns, and organize ideas before creating artwork. Together, these skills help artists and designers produce clear, effective, and visually appealing designs.

Composition is the arrangement of visual elements within a design or artwork. A well-composed image guides the viewer's eyes naturally, highlights important information, and creates a balanced and engaging visual experience.

Lesson illustration
Designers use composition principles in illustration, photography, graphic design, animation, user interface design, and many other creative fields.

Good composition helps communicate ideas clearly while improving both aesthetics and usability.

A focal point is the area that attracts the viewer's attention first. It is usually the most important element in the composition because it communicates the primary message.

Designers create focal points by using visual differences such as:

Larger size
Bright or contrasting colors
Unique shapes
Strong lighting
Strategic placement
An effective focal point helps viewers immediately understand where to look before exploring the rest of the composition.

Lesson illustration
The Rule of Thirds is a composition guideline that divides an image into nine equal sections using two equally spaced horizontal lines and two equally spaced vertical lines.

The four points where these lines intersect are considered strong visual positions for placing important subjects.

Using the Rule of Thirds can help designers:

Create balanced compositions.
Improve visual interest.
Avoid placing every subject directly in the center.
Leave appropriate empty space that makes the composition feel more natural.
This technique is widely used in photography, illustration, filmmaking, and digital design.

Visual hierarchy is the arrangement of elements according to their importance. It controls the order in which viewers notice information.

Lesson illustration
Designers establish visual hierarchy by adjusting:

Size
Color
Contrast
Typography
Position
Spacing
The most important information should stand out first, while supporting information should appear less prominent. A clear hierarchy improves readability and helps viewers understand content quickly.

Leading lines are lines or visual paths that naturally direct the viewer's eyes toward an important subject.

Lesson illustration
Leading lines may be created by:

Roads
Railways
Buildings
Rivers
Shadows
Architectural structures
Repeated objects
These lines help guide attention through the composition and emphasize important elements without using text.

Negative space is the empty area surrounding or between the main subjects in a composition. It is an intentional design element rather than unused space.

Lesson illustration
Negative space helps:

Highlight the main subject.
Improve readability.
Reduce visual clutter.
Create balance.
Make compositions appear cleaner and more organized.
Professional designers often use generous negative space to improve clarity and create elegant, modern designs.
