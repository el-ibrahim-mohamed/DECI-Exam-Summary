## :blue[🌐 Week 11: Living in a Digital World]

This section defines what it means to live in a digital world, how services have transformed, and the importance of protecting valuable online assets and data.

### :green[Definition of a Digital World]

A **digital world** is a society where people, organizations, and services use computers, networks, and the internet for communication, work, and access to information.

### :green[Digital Transformation]

**:green[Definition:]** :orange[Digital Transformation] is when businesses and people change old ways of doing things by using technology and the internet.

-   :yellow[**Example:**] Schools now use online learning platforms (e.g., Google Classroom) instead of only chalkboards and printed textbooks.

### :green[Connected Systems]

**:green[Definition:]** :orange[Connected systems] are devices and services that work together through the internet, sharing information automatically.

-   :yellow[**Example:**] A smart home where your phone controls lights and temperature, and devices share data.
-   :red[**Security Note:**] More connected devices increase security risks; use strong passwords and keep systems updated.

### :green[Digital Assets]

**:green[Definition:]** :orange[Digital assets] are valuable things that exist only online and are important to individuals or businesses.

-   **Personal Digital Assets:** Photos, emails, social media accounts, in-game items, documents.
-   **Business Digital Assets:** Customer databases, software, websites, business plans, employee records.
-   :orange[**Importance:**] Protecting digital assets prevents identity theft, financial loss, and reputational damage.

### :green[Personal and Organizational Data]

**:green[Definition of Data:]** Data is information about people and organizations, including numbers, text, images, videos, and location.

-   **Personal Data (Your Information):**
    -   **Identifying Information:** Name, age, address, phone number, email, Social Security number.
    -   **Behavioral Data:** Websites visited, searches, location, purchases.
    -   **Health Data:** Medical history, medications.
    -   **Financial Data:** Bank accounts, credit card numbers, salary.
-   **Organizational Data (Business Information):** Customer information, employee records, financial reports, business secrets, product designs.
-   :red[**Why Protection Matters:**] Businesses use data to improve services; hackers steal data for identity theft, fraud, or to disrupt operations.
-   **:key[How to Protect Data:]** Use strong passwords, avoid sharing sensitive info online, be careful with social media posts, don't click suspicious links, keep software updated.

## :blue[🛡️ Week 11: What is Cybersecurity]

This section defines cybersecurity, its core goals, and the essential elements (people, processes, technology) needed for effective protection, differentiating it from information security.

### :green[Definition of Cybersecurity]

:orange[**Cybersecurity**] is the practice of protecting digital systems (computers, networks, data) from theft, damage, or misuse by hackers and cybercriminals.

### :green[Security Goals: The CIA Triad]

The three main goals cybersecurity aims to achieve are **Confidentiality, Integrity,** and **Availability**.

1.  ### 🔒 :green[Confidentiality]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** Keeping information secret; only authorized individuals can access it.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] Your medical records are only accessible by your doctor and relevant medical staff, not by others.
    -   **:key[How to Protect:]** Passwords, encryption, privacy settings, access control.

2.  ### ✅ :green[Integrity]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** Ensuring information is accurate and has not been changed or corrupted without authorization.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] A doctor prescribes 10mg of medicine, and the record must remain 10mg, not 100mg.
    -   **:key[How to Protect:]** Digital signatures, checksums, backups, version control, audit trails.

3.  ###  uptime :green[Availability]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** Ensuring systems and information are accessible and functional when needed.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] Hospital systems must be operational 24/7 so doctors can access patient records for urgent care.
    -   **:key[How to Protect:]** System updates, redundancy, data backups, disaster recovery plans, monitoring, load balancing.

### :green[Three Pillars of Protection: People, Processes, Technology]

Effective security requires all three elements to work together.

1.  **Protecting People:**
    -   :red[**Problem:**] People are often the weakest link (weak passwords, clicking suspicious links).
    -   **:key[How to Protect:]** Cybersecurity training (phishing, social engineering awareness), fostering a security-aware culture.
2.  **Protecting Processes:**
    -   **:green[Definition:]** Steps and procedures followed to keep systems secure.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] A password management process: create strong passwords, change regularly, don't share.
    -   **:key[How to Protect:]** Clear procedures, training, regular checks, updates.
3.  **Protecting Technology:**
    -   **:green[Definition:]** Using technical tools and systems to defend against threats.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] Firewalls (control network traffic), antivirus software (scans for malware), encryption (turns info into code), Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA), backups.
    -   :orange[**Importance:**] All three must work together; a failure in one can compromise the others.

### :violet[Cybersecurity vs. Information Security]

| Concept                | Definition                                                    | What it Includes                                           | Key Difference                                              |
| :--------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------ | :--------------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Information Security** | Protecting ALL information (digital and physical) from misuse. | Digital data, physical documents, managing all info risks.   | Broader scope; protects all types of information.           |
| **Cybersecurity**      | Protecting specifically DIGITAL systems and data from cyber-attacks. | Defending against online threats, securing digital devices. | Narrower scope; protects digital information from cyber threats. |

## :blue[⚠️ Week 11: Why Cybersecurity Matters]

This section explains the significant consequences of cybersecurity failures across financial, privacy, business, and national security domains.

### :red[1. Financial Impact]

Cyber-attacks incur significant costs for individuals and organizations.

-   **Direct Costs:**
    -   :orange[**Ransomware Payments:**] Paying hackers to restore encrypted files (can be millions).
    -   :orange[**Fixing the Problem:**] Hiring experts, rebuilding systems, forensic analysis.
    -   :orange[**Legal & Fines:**] Notifying affected customers, credit monitoring, legal fees, government fines.
-   **Indirect Costs:**
    -   :orange[**Lost Business:**] Customers lose trust, move to competitors.
    -   :orange[**Reputation Damage:**] Negative news, stock price drops, investor loss of confidence.
    -   :orange[**Employee Disruption:**] Reduced productivity, low morale during breaches.

### :red[2. Personal Privacy Threats]

Cybersecurity failures expose personal data, leading to severe privacy violations.

-   **Information at Risk:** Identification details (name, SSN), behavioral data (browsing, location), photos, messages.
-   :red[**Consequence: Identity Theft:**] Criminals use stolen data to open credit cards, take loans in your name, ruining your credit score.

### :red[3. Business Continuity Risks]

Cyber-attacks can disrupt or halt business operations, leading to massive losses.

-   **System Downtime:**
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] A hospital system going down means doctors can't access patient records, endangering lives.
    -   :orange[**Cost:**] Millions in lost revenue per hour/minute, depending on the industry.
-   **Data Loss:** Ransomware can encrypt or delete critical files, halting production.
-   :yellow[**Real-World Example (Colonial Pipeline 2021):**] A ransomware attack shut down a major fuel pipeline, causing gas shortages and a national emergency, demonstrating critical infrastructure vulnerability.

### :red[4. National Security Concerns]

Cyber-attacks extend beyond businesses to threaten entire countries and critical infrastructure.

-   **Critical Infrastructure at Risk:**
    -   :orange[**Power Grid:**] Millions without electricity, disrupting hospitals, water, communications.
    -   :orange[**Water Systems:**] Potential for contamination, public health emergencies.
    -   :orange[**Transportation:**] Risk of accidents, plane crashes, traffic disruptions.
-   **Attacks by Other Countries:**
    -   :orange[**Espionage:**] Stealing military or government secrets.
    -   :orange[**Sabotage:**] Disabling power plants, disrupting supply chains.
    -   :orange[**Election Interference:**] Hacking voting systems, spreading misinformation.

### :red[Why Breaches Keep Happening]

-   **Not Updating Software:** Exploiting known vulnerabilities.
-   **Weak Passwords:** Easy to guess or crack.
-   **Phishing Emails:** Employees clicking suspicious links.
-   **Lack of Monitoring:** Breaches go undetected for long periods.
-   **Poor Access Control:** Too many people have access to sensitive data.

## :blue[🔐 Week 11: Core Security Principles: The CIA Triad]

The CIA Triad (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability) are the fundamental pillars of cybersecurity; if one fails, the entire security system is compromised.

### 🔒 :green[Confidentiality]

**:green[Definition:]** Keeping information private and secret, ensuring only authorized individuals can access it.

-   **:orange[Why it matters:]** Protects personal data (passwords, medical records) and business secrets (customer databases, trade secrets).
-   **:key[How to Protect:]**
    -   **Strong Passwords:** Unique, complex, not shared.
    -   **Encryption:** Converts information into code, unreadable without the key.
    -   **Access Control:** Limiting who can view information based on job necessity.
    -   **Privacy Settings:** Controlling who sees your online information.

### ✅ :green[Integrity]

**:green[Definition:]** Ensuring information remains accurate and unaltered without permission.

-   **:orange[Why it matters:]** Prevents harm from modified data (e.g., incorrect medicine dosage in healthcare) and financial fraud (e.g., altered transaction amounts).
-   **:key[How to Protect:]**
    -   **Digital Signatures:** Proof that information came from a legitimate source and hasn't been changed.
    -   **Checksums:** Mathematical checks to detect even slight data alterations.
    -   **Backups:** Copies of important data for restoration if corrupted.
    -   **Audit Trails:** Records of who accessed/changed information.

### uptime :green[Availability]

**:green[Definition:]** Ensuring systems and information are accessible and operational when needed.

-   **:orange[Why it matters:]** Prevents disruption of critical services (e.g., hospital systems, banking apps) and financial losses.
-   **:key[How to Protect:]**
    -   **Regular Updates:** Fix problems and prevent crashes.
    -   **Redundancy:** Backup systems that take over if the main system fails.
    -   **Data Backups:** Regular copies stored in different locations.
    -   **Disaster Recovery Plan:** Procedures for restoring operations after system failures.
    -   **Monitoring:** Watching systems for problems to fix them proactively.
    -   **Load Balancing:** Spreading traffic across multiple servers to prevent overload.

### :violet[The Balance Between CIA Principles]

Protecting one CIA principle can sometimes create trade-offs with another (e.g., strong encryption for confidentiality might slow performance, impacting availability). Finding the right balance is crucial.

## :blue[🧩 Week 11: Additional Security Concepts]

Beyond the CIA Triad, these four principles answer critical questions about identity, access, actions, and accountability.

### :green[Authentication]

**:green[Definition:]** Proving your identity to a system. It answers: "Are you really who you say you are?"

-   :yellow[**Example:**] Showing your ID at the airport to prove your identity before boarding a flight.
-   **:key[How it Works Online:]** Username and password.
-   :red[**Problem with Passwords Alone:**] Can be guessed, stolen, or hacked.
-   **Types of Authentication Methods (Factors):**
    1.  **Something You Know:** Passwords, PINs, security questions.
    2.  **Something You Have:** Physical key, ID card, phone (for codes), security token.
    3.  **Something You Are:** Biometrics (fingerprint, facial recognition).
-   **:key[Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA):]** Using two or more different authentication methods (e.g., password + phone code). Highly secure because an attacker needs multiple factors.
-   **:key[Strong Passwords:]** Long (12+ characters), mix of uppercase/lowercase, numbers, and symbols. Avoid common words or personal info. Use different passwords for different accounts.

### :green[Authorization]

**:green[Definition:]** Determining what an authenticated user is allowed to do. It answers: "What are you allowed to do?"

-   :orange[**Relation to Authentication:**] Authentication confirms *who* you are; authorization defines *what* you can do.
-   :yellow[**Example:**] After logging into a bank (authentication), you can withdraw up to $500, but cannot access other customers' accounts (authorization).
-   **:key[Role-Based Authorization:]** Access granted based on an individual's job role (e.g., a teacher sees all student grades, a student only sees their own).
-   **:key[Principle of Least Privilege:]** Users are given only the minimum access necessary to perform their job tasks to limit potential damage if an account is compromised.

### :green[Accountability]

**:green[Definition:]** Recording what people do so their actions can be tracked. It answers: "What did you do and when did you do it?"

-   **:orange[Why it matters:]** Without it, there's no record to investigate security incidents, prove access, or hold individuals responsible.
-   **:key[What Gets Recorded (Audit Logs):]**
    -   **Authentication Events:** Who logged in, when, from where, success/failure.
    -   **Data Access Events:** Who accessed what file, when, what action was taken (read, copied).
    -   **Administrative Actions:** Who changed permissions or settings.

### :green[Non-Repudiation]

**:green[Definition:]** Proof that an action was performed by a specific individual, preventing them from denying it. It answers: "Can you deny you did this action?" (No, you can't).

-   **:key[How it Works:]** Primarily through **digital signatures**, which are like electronic fingerprints for documents. The signature is mathematically tied to the document and proves the sender's identity and that the content hasn't been altered.
-   **:orange[Why it matters:]** Provides legal protection (e.g., for digital contracts), prevents fraud, and enforces accountability.

### :violet[Complete Security Example: Bank Transfer]

1.  **Authentication:** You log in with password + fingerprint (proves "you're Sarah Johnson").
2.  **Authorization:** System checks your access (you can transfer $500, not access other accounts).
3.  **Accountability:** You initiate transfer; system logs: who, what, when, from/to accounts.
4.  **Non-Repudiation:** Transfer is digitally signed, proving you authorized it; you cannot later deny it.

## :blue[🎯 Week 11: Assets and Attack Surfaces]

This section defines valuable assets, identifies potential entry points for attackers, and explains how an individual's online presence can increase their risk.

### :green[Asset]

**:green[Definition:]** Anything of value that needs protection, whether physical or digital.

-   :yellow[**Example:**] Your social media account is a digital asset containing personal information.

### :green[Types of Assets]

Organizations must protect various categories of assets, each with different values and risks.

1.  **Information Assets:** Data and information (e.g., customer lists, trade secrets, personal details).
2.  **Technology Assets:** Computers, devices, software, infrastructure (e.g., laptops, servers, operating systems).
3.  **People Assets:** Employees, their knowledge, and skills.
4.  **Reputation Assets:** Brand image, customer trust, personal image.
5.  **Physical Assets:** Tangible items (e.g., office buildings, servers, USB drives).
-   **:orange[Asset Valuation:]** Assets are often prioritized as critical, high, medium, or low importance based on their value and impact if compromised.

### :red[Attack Surface]

**:green[Definition:]** The sum of all possible points where an unauthorized user can try to enter or extract data from a system.

-   :yellow[**Analogy:**] A house with many doors and windows has a larger attack surface, making it harder to defend.
-   **Components of an Attack Surface:**
    -   **Network Entry Points:** Routers, public-facing servers, email systems.
    -   **Software Vulnerabilities:** Outdated software, bugs in code, third-party apps.
    -   **User Behavior Entry Points:** Weak passwords, phishing, social engineering.
    -   **Physical Entry Points:** Unlocked doors, unattended devices, insecure public Wi-Fi.
-   **:key[Reducing Attack Surface:]**
    -   **Keep Software Updated:** Patches fix flaws.
    -   **Remove Unnecessary Software/Services:** Fewer potential vulnerabilities.
    -   **Use Firewalls:** Block unnecessary traffic.
    -   **Strong Passwords & MFA:** Harder to guess/crack.
    -   **Principle of Least Privilege:** Limit access to what's strictly needed.
    -   **Network Segmentation:** Separate critical networks to contain breaches.

### :red[Digital Footprint]

**:green[Definition:]** All the information about you that exists online, left behind as you use the internet.

-   **Components:** Emails, social media posts, online accounts, location data, purchase history, public records.
-   **Active Footprint:** Information you intentionally share (posts, accounts).
-   **Passive Footprint:** Data collected about you without your direct input (browsing cookies, location tracking).
-   **:red[Why it matters:]**
    -   **Security Risk:** Hackers use it for social engineering, password guessing.
    -   **Privacy Risk:** Companies collect/sell data about you.
    -   **Reputation Risk:** Old posts can be used against you (permanent record).
-   **:key[Managing Your Digital Footprint:]**
    -   **Reduce What You Share:** Be selective, avoid sensitive info.
    -   **Use Privacy Settings:** Set social media to private, adjust phone/browser settings.
    -   **Be Aware of Data Collection:** Understand what's tracked, use VPNs, privacy-focused search engines.
    -   **Regular Cleanup:** Delete old posts, unused accounts, search your name.

## :blue[💼 Week 11: Cybersecurity Career Tracks]

Cybersecurity is a diverse field with many specialized roles, each focusing on different aspects of protection.

-   **SOC Analyst:** Monitors systems 24/7 for attacks, investigates alerts, and escalates incidents. (Like a security guard).
-   **Incident Responder:** Handles active security breaches, identifies how hackers got in, contains damage, and recovers systems. (Like an investigator).
-   **Threat Intelligence Analyst:** Researches cyber threats, tracks hacker groups, predicts attacks, and reports findings to help organizations prepare. (Like a researcher/forecaster).
-   **Penetration Tester (Ethical Hacker):** Tries to hack into systems to find vulnerabilities *before* real attackers do. (Like a simulated attacker).
-   **Digital Forensics Specialist:** Investigates digital crimes by examining computers and devices to find evidence. (Like a detective).
-   **Application Security (AppSec) Specialist:** Ensures software is built securely, finds and fixes flaws in code before release. (Like a secure developer).
-   **Cloud Security Specialist:** Protects infrastructure and data deployed on cloud platforms (AWS, Azure, Google Cloud). (Like a cloud architect).
-   **GRC Specialist (Governance, Risk, and Compliance):** Manages organizational risks, ensures compliance with regulations, and develops security policies. (Like a strategic planner).

## :blue[💎 Week 12: Understanding Assets]

Understanding what assets an organization possesses is the crucial first step in building effective cybersecurity defenses.

### :green[Asset Definition]

An **asset** is anything of value to an organization that requires protection from cyberattacks and security threats.

### :green[Five Main Types of Assets]

1.  ### 📚 :green[Data]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** Information in various forms (text, numbers, images, videos) and is often the primary target for attackers.
    -   **Types:** Personal data (SSN, medical records), Business data (customer lists, trade secrets), Government data (classified info).
    -   **:red[How Data Gets Attacked:]** Theft, corruption, deletion, exposure.

2.  ### 🖥️ :green[Systems]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** The computers, servers, and devices that process and store data.
    -   **Types:** Personal computers, web/database servers, mobile devices, specialized systems (ATMs, IoT devices).
    -   **:red[How Systems Get Attacked:]** Malware infection, exploitation of vulnerabilities, Denial of Service (DoS), physical damage.

3.  ### ⚙️ :green[Applications]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** Software programs that run on systems and perform specific functions.
    -   **Types:** Productivity apps (Word), Business apps (CRM, ERP), Web apps (social media, banking), Security apps (firewalls, antivirus), Custom apps.
    -   **:red[How Applications Get Attacked:]** Code vulnerabilities, SQL Injection, Cross-Site Scripting (XSS), weak authentication.

4.  ### 🔌 :green[Infrastructure]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** The foundational physical and network components that everything else depends on.
    -   **Types:** Network infrastructure (routers, switches, firewalls), Physical infrastructure (data centers, server rooms, power supplies), Cloud infrastructure.
    -   **:red[How Infrastructure Gets Attacked:]** DDoS attacks, network interception, physical attacks (cutting cables), configuration issues.

5.  ### 🧍 :green[Human Assets]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** People are assets because they use and operate systems, make decisions, and can be manipulated.
    -   **Types:** Employees, their knowledge/skills, customers (users).
    -   **:red[How Human Assets Get Compromised:]** Social engineering, phishing, malware (via infected employee devices), blackmail.

### :orange[Asset Inventory and Valuation]

Organizations must list all their assets (**inventory**) and assess their **value** (cost to replace, impact if compromised, regulatory importance) to prioritize protection efforts (e.g., critical, high, medium, low value assets).

## :blue[⚠️ Week 12: Threats, Vulnerabilities, Risks, and Attacks]

These four fundamental cybersecurity concepts are distinct yet interconnected, explaining how harm can occur and how it can be prevented.

-   :orange[**Analogy:**] Think of home security:
    -   **Threat:** A burglar.
    -   **Vulnerability:** An unlocked window.
    -   **Risk:** The possibility the burglar will rob you via the window.
    -   **Attack:** The burglar breaking in through the window.

### :red[Threat]

**:green[Definition:]** Anything that could potentially harm your systems, data, or organization. It's the danger itself, often with malicious intent.

-   **:orange[Key Idea:]** A threat exists whether or not you have defenses.
-   **Examples:** Cybercriminals, nation-state actors, natural disasters (earthquakes), careless employees (accidents), malicious competitors.
-   **Types:** Internal (disgruntled employees, accidental mistakes), External (hackers, foreign governments).

### :red[Vulnerability]

**:green[Definition:]** A weakness or flaw in your systems, applications, or processes that can be exploited by a threat.

-   **:orange[Key Idea:]** A vulnerability exists whether or not anyone exploits it.
-   **Examples:** Unpatched software, weak passwords, misconfigured firewalls, untrained employees, unlocked physical doors, lack of security procedures.
-   :yellow[**Example:**] Unpatched software with a known security flaw is a vulnerability. Applying the patch removes it.

### :red[Risk]

**:green[Definition:]** The probability and impact of a threat exploiting a vulnerability. It combines the likelihood of something bad happening with the damage it would cause.

-   **:orange[Key Idea:]** Risk depends on the presence of a threat, a vulnerability, and the potential impact.
-   **Risk Formula:** `Risk = Threat x Vulnerability x Impact`
-   **Risk Assessment:** Organizations prioritize risks (high, medium, low) to decide where to focus protection.

### :red[Attack]

**:green[Definition:]** When a threat takes active, deliberate action to exploit a vulnerability and attempt to cause harm.

-   **:orange[Key Idea:]** An attack is an active event, an intentional attempt to cause harm.
-   **Examples:** A malware infection, a phishing attempt, a DDoS attack, a data breach.

### :violet[The Relationship & Prevention]

-   A **Threat** (e.g., hacker) looks for a **Vulnerability** (e.g., unpatched software).
-   If a vulnerability exists, there is a **Risk** (e.g., data breach).
-   An **Attack** is the actual exploitation of the vulnerability by the threat.
-   **:key[Prevention:]** The most effective way to reduce risk is to **eliminate or reduce vulnerabilities** (e.g., apply patches, use strong passwords) rather than trying to eliminate all threats (which is often impossible).

## :blue[🎭 Week 12: Threat Actors]

Behind every cyber threat is a person or group called a threat actor, whose motivations and capabilities dictate defense strategies.

### :green[Threat Actor Definition]

A **threat actor** is the person or group responsible for creating or carrying out a cyber threat. Identifying them helps in defense planning.

### :violet[Types of Threat Actors]

1.  ### 💰 :green[Cybercriminals]
    -   **Motivation:** Primarily **financial gain** (stealing money, selling data, ransomware).
    -   **Note:** Most widespread threat actor due to direct profit motive.

2.  ### 🏛️ :green[Nation-State Actors]
    -   **Motivation:** **Political or military aims** (espionage, sabotage of critical infrastructure, intelligence gathering).
    -   **Note:** Most dangerous and capable, backed by significant resources. Cybersecurity is part of national security because of them.

3.  ### 📢 :green[Hacktivists]
    -   **Motivation:** To support a **cause or deliver a message** (e.g., website defacement, leaking information as protest).
    -   **Note:** Not motivated by money, but their actions are illegal.

4.  ### 👤 :green[Insider Threats]
    -   **Motivation:** Varies; can be **deliberate** (disgruntled employee) or **accidental** (careless employee).
    -   **Note:** Especially dangerous as they already have legitimate access and permissions. Employee awareness training is key protection.

5.  ### 👶 :green[Script Kiddies]
    -   **Motivation:** Usually **curiosity or showing off** their capabilities.
    -   **Note:** Inexperienced attackers who use ready-made tools created by others, often without full understanding. Least dangerous in skill, but numerous.

## :blue[🔄 Week 12: Attack Lifecycle]

An attack unfolds through a series of predictable stages, which defenders study to intercept and stop attacks as early as possible.

### :green[Purpose of Studying the Lifecycle]

Understanding the **attack lifecycle** allows defenders to stop an attack at any stage before completion, reducing potential damage.

### :violet[The Six Stages of an Attack Lifecycle]

1.  ### 🕵️ :green[Reconnaissance]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** The attacker gathers information about the target to find weaknesses.
    -   :yellow[**Burglar Example:**] Watching a building, learning guard schedules, identifying doors.
    -   **:orange[Connection:]** Relates to a target's digital footprint (publicly available information).

2.  ### 🚪 :green[Initial Access]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** The attacker gains the first entry into the system.
    -   :yellow[**Burglar Example:**] Finding and entering through an open door or window.
    -   **:orange[Common Methods:]** Deceiving an employee (phishing), exploiting a weak password. Early detection here is crucial.

3.  ### 🏃 :green[Execution]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** The attacker begins to run malicious programs (malware) or carry out actions within the system.
    -   :yellow[**Burglar Example:**] Searching the building for valuables.

4.  ### 🗝️ :green[Persistence]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** The attacker creates a way to return to the system, even if the initial entry point is closed.
    -   :yellow[**Burglar Example:**] Making a copy of a key for future access.
    -   **:orange[Digital Domain:]** Leaving a hidden back door.

5.  ### 🚶 :green[Lateral Movement]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** The attacker moves from the initially compromised device to other devices and systems within the same network.
    -   :yellow[**Burglar Example:**] Moving from room to room searching for the most valuable items.
    -   **:orange[Importance:]** Monitoring network activity helps detect this unusual spread.

6.  ### 📤 :green[Exfiltration]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** The attacker transfers the stolen data out of the target's network to their own control.
    -   :yellow[**Burglar Example:**] Carrying stolen valuables out of the building.
    -   **:orange[Impact:]** This is where the real harm (data theft, extortion) is realized.

## :blue[🎣 Week 12: Social Engineering Attacks]

Social engineering manipulates people, rather than machines, by exploiting human emotions and trust to gain access or information.

### :green[Social Engineering Definition]

**:green[Definition:]** A method of attack that manipulates a person's emotions and trust, persuading the victim to hand over information or grant access willingly.

-   **:orange[Key Idea:]** Targets people, not technology, making it crucial for users to recognize and defend against.

### :violet[Five Forms of Social Engineering]

1.  ### 📧 :green[Phishing]
    -   **Method:** Fraudulent message (usually email) from a trusted source (e.g., bank, well-known company).
    -   **Purpose:** Deceive recipient into clicking a harmful link or entering credentials on a fake website.
    -   **Warning Signs:** Urgency/fear, spelling mistakes, unfamiliar sender, incorrect links.
    -   **:key[Protection:]** Never click links in unverified messages; only enter passwords on official websites you open personally.

2.  ### 🎯 :green[Spear Phishing]
    -   **Method:** Phishing specifically aimed at one person, using tailored information about the target.
    -   **Key Feature:** Appears highly convincing due to personalization (e.g., addresses victim by name, refers to their school or interests).

3.  ### 📞 :green[Vishing (Voice Phishing)]
    -   **Method:** Deception carried out through a telephone call.
    -   **Key Feature:** Attacker claims to be from a trusted organization (e.g., bank, tech support) and asks for sensitive info (e.g., card number, password).
    -   **:key[Protection:]** A genuine organization will *never* ask for your password or secret code over the phone. End the call and contact the official organization directly.

4.  ### 💬 :green[Smishing (SMS Phishing)]
    -   **Method:** Deception through a text message (SMS) containing a harmful link or request for information.
    -   **Key Feature:** Often promises prizes or warns of urgent problems.
    -   **:key[Protection:]** Similar to phishing: don't click suspicious links in texts, especially if they create urgency or offer something too good to be true.

5.  ### 🎭 :green[Pretexting]
    -   **Method:** The attacker invents a false story or identity to build trust and extract information.
    -   **Key Feature:** Constructs a complete, convincing scenario (e.g., fake IT support calling to "help" with an account problem by asking for a password).
    -   **:key[Protection:]** Always verify a person's identity through official sources before providing any information, regardless of how convincing their story seems.

## :blue[🦠 Week 12: Malware Attacks]

Malware, or malicious software, refers to harmful programs designed to damage devices or steal data, taking various forms to achieve different objectives.

### :green[Malware Definition]

**:green[Definition:]** Any harmful program (**mal**icious soft**ware**) created to damage a device, disrupt operations, or steal data.

### :violet[Five Forms of Malware]

1.  ### 👾 :green[Virus]
    -   **Behavior:** Attaches itself to a legitimate file or program and spreads when that file is run or passed to others.
    -   **Key Feature:** Requires a user action (e.g., opening an infected file) to spread.

2.  ### 🐛 :green[Worm]
    -   **Behavior:** Spreads by itself across a network without needing a file attachment or user action.
    -   **Key Feature:** Copies itself rapidly from one device to another, making it fast and dangerous.

3.  ### 🐎 :green[Trojan (Trojan Horse)]
    -   **Behavior:** Disguises itself as a legitimate and useful program, tricking users into installing it.
    -   **Key Feature:** Appears safe on the outside but conceals harmful functions (e.g., opens a backdoor for attackers).
    -   **:key[Protection:]** Download programs and games only from official, trusted sources.

4.  ### 👁️ :green[Spyware]
    -   **Behavior:** Secretly observes user activity and collects information without their knowledge (e.g., records keystrokes, captures screen images, tracks websites).
    -   **Key Feature:** Operates silently in the background; a device may appear normal while being monitored.
    -   **Signs:** Device suddenly slows down, unusual internet activity.
    -   **:key[Protection:]** Use antivirus software, avoid suspicious programs.

5.  ### 🔒 :green[Ransomware]
    -   **Behavior:** Locks a user's files or entire device by encrypting data and demands a payment (ransom) to unlock them.
    -   **Key Feature:** Openly demands payment and halts operations. One of the most dangerous forms.
    -   **:key[Protection:]** The most important defense is to **keep regular backup copies of your files** in a separate location.

### :key[Common Malware Protections]

-   Antivirus software
-   Keeping software updated
-   Downloading only from trusted sources
-   Regular data backups

## :blue[🌐 Week 12: Network-Based Attacks]

These attacks target the connections and infrastructure of networks, threatening the CIA Triad principles.

### :green[Definition]

**Network-based attacks** target the connections and underlying infrastructure of a network, exploiting vulnerabilities in how devices communicate over the internet.

### :violet[Four Forms of Network-Based Attacks]

1.  ### 💥 :red[Denial-of-Service (DoS) / Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDoS) Attack]
    -   **What it Does:** Floods a website or server with an enormous volume of false requests, overwhelming it until it goes offline.
    -   **CIA Principle Threatened:** **Availability** (prevents legitimate users from accessing the service).

2.  ### 🤝 :red[Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) Attack]
    -   **What it Does:** The attacker secretly intercepts and relays communication between two parties, reading and potentially altering information without their knowledge.
    -   **CIA Principles Threatened:** **Confidentiality** (attacker reads messages) and **Integrity** (attacker can change messages).
    -   **:key[Protection:]** Use websites with **HTTPS** (the 'S' indicates encryption), avoid sensitive activities on public Wi-Fi.

3.  ### 🗺️ :red[DNS Attack (Domain Name System Attack)]
    -   **What it Does:** The attacker tampers with the internet's "telephone directory" (DNS), redirecting users typing a genuine website address to a counterfeit site.
    -   **CIA Principles Threatened:** **Confidentiality** (user enters info on fake site) and **Integrity** (directory altered).
    -   **:key[Protection:]** Always verify the website address (URL) and look for the HTTPS indicator before entering sensitive information.

4.  ### 📡 :red[Packet Sniffing]
    -   **What it Does:** An attacker captures and reads small pieces of data (**packets**) as they travel across a network.
    -   **CIA Principle Threatened:** **Confidentiality** (if data is unencrypted, attacker can read messages and passwords).
    -   **:key[Protection:]** **Encryption** (makes captured data unreadable); avoid public Wi-Fi for sensitive tasks.

## :blue[🛡️ Week 13: Introduction to Security Controls]

Security controls are the primary defense mechanisms organizations use to protect assets and manage risks.

### :green[Security Control Definition]

A **security control** is any measure or means implemented to protect assets and reduce risk. It makes an attacker's task more difficult.

-   **:orange[Purpose:]** To maintain Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability (CIA) by preventing unauthorized access, detecting harmful activity, and supporting recovery.
-   :yellow[**Examples:**] A password on a phone, a lock on a door, not opening unfamiliar links.

### :green[Risk Reduction]

-   **:orange[Key Idea:]** Controls close vulnerabilities, thereby reducing risk.
-   :red[**Important:**] The goal is **risk reduction**, not elimination; perfect safety is impossible.

### :green[Security Management & Defence in Depth]

-   **Security Management:** The organized process of choosing, applying, monitoring, and updating controls based on asset importance and risk.
-   **Defence in Depth:** A central principle meaning **layered protection**. Multiple security layers are placed one behind another so that if one fails, the next layer can stop the attacker.
    -   :yellow[**Analogy:**] An old castle with a moat, high walls, guards, and gates.

## :blue[📋 Week 13: Administrative Controls]

Administrative controls focus on people and rules, guiding employee behavior to enhance security.

### :green[Administrative Controls Definition]

**:green[Definition:]** Security controls concerned with people and rules, such as policies, procedures, and training, to organize employee behavior safely.

-   **:orange[Role:]** Strengthen the "People" pillar of security. Also include governance and compliance.

### :violet[Four Key Administrative Controls]

1.  ### 📜 :green[Policy]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** A general rule that states what is required or not permitted (e.g., "All employees must use strong passwords").
    -   **Key Feature:** Sets the goal, but not the detailed "how."

2.  ### 📝 :green[Procedure]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** A detailed set of steps explaining *exactly how* a policy is to be carried out (e.g., "How to report a suspicious email: 1. Don't click, 2. Send to security, 3. Delete").
    -   **Key Feature:** Provides the method, ensuring consistent correct action.

3.  ### 🧑‍🏫 :green[Security Awareness Training]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** Instruction given to employees to help them recognize threats (like phishing) and respond correctly.
    -   **Key Feature:** Directly addresses human vulnerabilities, making employees the strongest defense. Most effective when continuous.

4.  ### 📏 :green[Standard]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** A specific and mandatory requirement that converts a general policy into a precise condition (e.g., a "strong password" *standard* might be "at least 12 characters, with numbers and symbols").
    -   **Key Feature:** Removes ambiguity from policies by setting exact requirements.

## :blue[💻 Week 13: Technical Controls]

Technical controls are technological tools and programs that directly protect systems and data from attackers.

### :green[Technical Controls Definition]

**:green[Definition:]** Tools and programs that protect systems and data by technological means, standing directly in the path of an attacker.

### :violet[Five Key Technical Controls]

1.  ### 🧱 :green[Firewall]
    -   **Function:** Controls network connections, deciding which are permitted to pass into or out of a network and blocking suspicious ones.
    -   **Defends Against:** Harmful network connections.

2.  ### 🛡️ :green[Antivirus]
    -   **Function:** Detects and removes known malware (viruses, Trojans, spyware) from a device.
    -   **Defends Against:** Known malware and viruses.

3.  ### 💻 :green[EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response)]
    -   **Function:** An advanced system installed on endpoint devices (laptops, phones) that continuously monitors device behavior, detects unusual activity (even from unknown programs), and responds (e.g., isolates an infected device).
    -   **Defends Against:** New and unknown threats, active attacks.
    -   **:orange[Difference from Antivirus:]** EDR goes beyond simple detection/removal to continuous monitoring, investigation, and response.

4.  ### 🔑 :green[MFA (Multi-Factor Authentication)]
    -   **Function:** Requires a second proof of identity beyond a password (e.g., password + phone code or fingerprint).
    -   **Defends Against:** Stolen passwords, unauthorized account access.
    -   **:orange[Importance:]** Highly effective, even if a password is compromised, the second factor blocks entry.

5.  ### 📊 :green[SIEM (Security Information and Event Management)]
    -   **Function:** Collects security records and events from all devices and tools across an organization into one central place and analyzes them together.
    -   **Defends Against:** Hidden or coordinated attacks by linking seemingly unrelated events.
    -   **:orange[Role:]** Supports security analysts by providing a complete picture for investigation.

## :blue[🔒 Week 13: Physical Controls]

Physical controls protect tangible assets and facilities, preventing unauthorized physical access to systems and data.

### :green[Physical Controls Definition]

**:green[Definition:]** Security controls that protect physical objects, facilities, and equipment.

-   **:orange[Why they matter:]** An attacker with physical access can bypass software defenses (e.g., stealing a server).

### :violet[Four Key Physical Controls]

1.  ### 👮 :green[Security Guards]
    -   **Role:** Human line of defense at entrances; they watch, question, and prevent entry of unauthorized or suspicious individuals.
    -   **Related Principle:** Prevention and real-time response.

2.  ### 📸 :green[CCTV (Closed-Circuit Television)]
    -   **Role:** Cameras that record activity for deterrence and to provide evidence if an incident occurs.
    -   **Related Principle:** **Accountability** (records who did what, when).

3.  ### 🔐 :green[Locks]
    -   **Role:** Devices that secure doors and sensitive rooms (e.g., server rooms), directly preventing physical access to valuable devices.
    -   **Related Principle:** **Confidentiality** (protects physical access to data).

4.  ### 🆔 :green[Access Badges]
    -   **Role:** Cards carried by employees that determine exactly where they are permitted to enter. They also record who entered which place and when.
    -   **Related Principles:** **Authorization** (controls who may enter where) and **Accountability** (records entries).

## :blue[⚙️ Week 13: Control Functions]

Controls can also be classified by *what* they do and *when* they act relative to an attack.

### :green[Control Functions Definition]

**:green[Definition:]** A classification of controls based on their purpose and timing in relation to an attack (before, during, or after).

### :violet[Five Control Functions]

1.  ### 🚫 :green[Preventive Control]
    -   **Timing:** **Before** an attack occurs.
    -   **Purpose:** To stop an attack from happening at all.
    -   :yellow[**Examples:**] Locks, firewalls, MFA, employee security training.

2.  ### 🚨 :green[Detective Control]
    -   **Timing:** **During** or **after** an attack has occurred.
    -   **Purpose:** To detect that an attack is taking place or has happened, giving a warning.
    -   :yellow[**Examples:**] CCTV, alerting systems, system logs, SIEM.

3.  ### 🩹 :green[Corrective Control]
    -   **Timing:** **After** an attack has been detected.
    -   **Purpose:** To stop the damage and repair the immediate problem.
    -   :yellow[**Examples:**] Removing malware, isolating an infected device, closing a compromised account.

4.  ### 🔄 :green[Recovery Control]
    -   **Timing:** **After** an attack has been stopped.
    -   **Purpose:** To restore systems and data to their normal state, ensuring **Availability**.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] Restoring data from a backup after a ransomware attack.
    -   **:orange[Distinction:]** Corrective ends the harm; Recovery restores what was lost.

5.  ### 🛠️ :green[Compensating Control]
    -   **Timing:** Used **when the ideal control cannot be applied**.
    -   **Purpose:** An alternative measure or substitute that makes up for the absence of a primary control.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] Placing an additional security guard if an alarm system is broken until it can be repaired.

## :blue[📡 Week 13: Monitoring and Detection]

Effective defense relies on continuous monitoring and the systematic detection of threats, differentiating real dangers from false alarms.

### :green[Detection Process]

Detection works through a logical sequence of four steps, from recording events to identifying genuine threats.

### :violet[Four Steps of Detection]

1.  ### ✍️ :green[Logging]
    -   **What it Does:** Records every event that occurs within a system into a **log** file (who entered, when, what was opened, what was done).
    -   **:orange[Key Idea:]** The foundation of detection; nothing can be detected without a record. Connects to **Accountability**.

2.  ### 🔔 :green[Alerting]
    -   **What it Does:** Automatic notification sent by a system when it finds something suspicious in the logs (e.g., 50 incorrect login attempts in one minute).
    -   :yellow[**Analogy:**] A fire alarm that sounds automatically when danger is sensed.

3.  ### 👁️‍🗨️ :green[Security Monitoring]
    -   **What it Does:** The continuous, around-the-clock observation of systems and alerts by a team or system.
    -   :yellow[**Analogy:**] A guard in a control room watching all cameras for unusual movement.

4.  ### 🔍 :green[Threat Detection]
    -   **What it Does:** The analysis of alerts and data to distinguish a genuine threat from a false alarm.
    -   **:orange[Key Skill:]** Judgment is crucial to avoid wasting effort on false alarms while not missing real attacks.
    -   :yellow[**Analogy:**] A doctor examining test results to distinguish normal from urgent.

## :blue[🖥️ Week 13: Security Operations Center (SOC)]

The Security Operations Center (SOC) is the central team and facility where cybersecurity defenses are put into continuous practice.

### :green[SOC Purpose]

A **Security Operations Center (SOC)** is a central team dedicated to **monitoring, detecting, and responding** to cyber threats around the clock.

-   :yellow[**Analogy:**] A central control room or fire station.
-   **:orange[Key Tool:]** The **SIEM** (Security Information and Event Management) system is often central to the SOC, gathering and correlating events from across the organization.

### :violet[SOC Workflow]

The SOC operates through an ordered sequence of steps:

1.  **Monitoring:** Continuously watching screens and logs for activity.
2.  **Detection:** Noticing an alert about suspicious activity.
3.  **Investigation:** Examining the alert to determine if it's a real threat or a false alarm.
4.  **Response:** If a real threat is confirmed, acting to stop the attack and limit damage.
5.  **Recovery:** After the attack is stopped, restoring systems to normal and learning lessons to prevent future incidents.

### :green[Security Analyst Responsibilities]

The **security analyst** is the person working within the SOC, forming the first line of defense.

-   Continuously watches screens and alerts.
-   Sorts alerts to prioritize important ones.
-   Investigates alerts to understand what is happening.
-   Escalates large attacks to specialized teams (e.g., incident response).
-   Documents incidents and helps improve detection rules.

## :blue[📖 Week 14: Introduction to Basic Design]

Art and design, though related, serve distinct purposes: art for expression, design for problem-solving and clear communication.

### :green[Art Definition]

**:green[Definition:]** A form of creative expression for thoughts, emotions, and imagination. It is often subjective and lacks a specific practical purpose.

### :green[Design Definition]

**:green[Definition:]** The process of creating visual solutions to fulfill a specific purpose or solve a problem, with a clear audience and objective in mind. It prioritizes clarity, functionality, and visual appeal.

### :violet[Art vs. Design]

| Feature           | Art                                     | Design                                            |
| :---------------- | :-------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------ |
| **Primary Goal**  | Express ideas and emotions              | Solve problems and communicate information        |
| **Interpretation**| Personal, open to multiple meanings     | Delivers a clear, intended message                |
| **Focus**         | Creative expression                     | Usability and effectiveness                       |

### :green[Visual Communication]

**:green[Definition:]** Conveying information through images, symbols, colors, and typography, rather than solely relying on text.

-   **:orange[Importance:]** Good design allows quick understanding due to widely recognized visual symbols.
-   :yellow[**Examples:**] A checkmark (✓) for correct action, an X (✕) for error, a trash bin icon for deleting.

### :green[The Design Process (Iterative)]

Design is an iterative process of refinement through multiple stages:

1.  **Understanding the Problem:** Identify user needs, goals, requirements.
2.  **Generating Ideas:** Brainstorming, rough sketches, exploring solutions.
3.  **Creating a Prototype:** Detailed version using software.
4.  **Collecting Feedback:** Review by clients/users for improvements.
5.  **Refining the Design:** Revisions based on feedback until goals are met.

## :blue[💡 Week 14: Elements of Art]

The elements of art are the fundamental visual components used to build any artwork, guiding the viewer's attention and communicating ideas.

### :green[Elements of Art Definition]

**:green[Definition:]** The basic building blocks used to create visual compositions. These include **Line, Shape, Form, Value,** and **Texture.**

### :violet[Primary Elements of Art]

1.  ### 〰️ :green[Line]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** The path created when a point moves across a surface.
    -   **Function:** Defines shapes, separates spaces, suggests movement, guides the eye.
    -   **Types:** Straight, curved, horizontal, vertical, diagonal, thick, thin.

2.  ### 🔵 :green[Shape]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** A two-dimensional (height, width) area enclosed by lines or edges.
    -   **Types:**
        -   **Geometric Shapes:** Regular, structured (circle, square, triangle).
        -   **Organic Shapes:** Irregular, nature-inspired (leaves, clouds).
    -   **Function:** Foundation for complex designs.

3.  ### 🧊 :green[Form]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** A three-dimensional (height, width, depth) object, created by adding the illusion of volume to a shape.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] A circle becomes a sphere; a square becomes a cube.
    -   **Function:** Creates realism using perspective, lighting, and shading.

4.  ### 🌑 :green[Value]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** The lightness or darkness of a color or object, created by highlights and shadows.
    -   **Function:** Creates depth, shows volume, suggests lighting, makes objects appear 3D.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] Silhouettes are created using value (dark outline against a brighter background) to emphasize shape.

5.  ### 🎨 :green[Texture]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** Describes how a surface looks or feels (actual or implied).
    -   :yellow[**Examples:**] Smooth, rough, soft, hard, metallic.
    -   **Function:** Adds realism and visual interest to artwork.

### :orange[Elements Work Together]

Artists combine multiple elements (e.g., thick/thin lines, light/dark values, different shapes/forms) to create balanced, engaging, and meaningful compositions.

## :blue[📐 Week 14: Principles of Design and Gestalt Principles]

Principles of Design are guidelines for organizing visual elements, while Gestalt Principles explain how people naturally interpret visual information.

### :green[Principles of Design]

**:green[Definition:]** Guidelines that help organize visual elements into clear, effective, attractive, and understandable compositions.

1.  ### ⚖️ :green[Balance]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** Visual distribution of elements for stability and comfort.
    -   **Types:**
        -   **Symmetrical:** Evenly arranged on both sides of an axis.
        -   **Asymmetrical:** Different-sized elements create equilibrium.
        -   **Radial:** Elements arranged around a central point.

2.  ### ↔️ :green[Contrast]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** Placing different visual elements together to make important information stand out.
    -   :yellow[**Examples:**] Light vs. dark colors, large vs. small sizes, bold vs. regular text.
    -   **Function:** Improves readability, attracts attention.

3.  ### 🌟 :green[Emphasis]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** Making one element the main point of focus, directing the viewer's attention first.
    -   **How to Create:** Larger size, bright/contrasting colors, bold typography, strategic placement, extra spacing.

4.  ### ➡️ :green[Movement]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** The visual path that guides the viewer's eyes through a design.
    -   **How to Create:** Directional lines, curved shapes, repeated patterns, gradual changes, object positioning.

5.  ### 🤝 :green[Unity]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** The sense that all parts of a design belong together, forming one complete visual message.
    -   **How to Achieve:** Consistency in color, typography, shapes, spacing, visual themes.

6.  ### 📏 :green[Proportion]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** The relationship between the sizes of different elements within a composition.
    -   **Function:** Makes objects realistic or can be exaggerated for artistic style/communication.

### :green[Gestalt Principles]

**:green[Definition:]** Psychological concepts explaining how people naturally organize visual information into meaningful patterns by grouping related elements.

1.  ### 🗂️ :green[Proximity]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** Objects placed close together are perceived as belonging to the same group.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] Buttons grouped in a navigation menu are seen as related options.

2.  ### 👯 :green[Similarity]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** Objects sharing similar characteristics (color, shape, size, texture) are perceived as belonging together.
    -   :yellow[**Example:**] Navigation buttons with the same color and shape.

3.  ### 🛣️ :green[Continuation]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** The eye tends to follow lines, curves, or patterns smoothly, even when interrupted.
    -   **Function:** Guides users through layouts, creates natural reading flow.

4.  ### ⭕ :green[Closure]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** The tendency to mentally complete incomplete shapes or figures.
    -   **Function:** Used in logos to suggest shapes without drawing every outline.

5.  ### 🖼️ :green[Figure–Ground]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** How people distinguish the main subject (figure) from its background (ground).
    -   **Function:** Creates a clear distinction between important content and surrounding space.

## :blue[🖼️ Week 14: Composition and Visual Thinking]

Composition is the art of arranging visual elements to guide attention and communicate clearly, supported by visual thinking for effective design.

### :green[Composition Definition]

**:green[Definition:]** The arrangement of visual elements within a design to guide the viewer's eyes, highlight important information, and create a balanced and engaging visual experience.

### :violet[Key Composition Techniques]

1.  ### 🎯 :green[Focal Point]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** The area that first attracts the viewer's attention and communicates the primary message.
    -   **How to Create:** Larger size, bright/contrasting colors, unique shapes, strong lighting, strategic placement.

2.  ### 📏 :green[Rule of Thirds]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** A guideline dividing an image into nine equal sections by two horizontal and two vertical lines. Important subjects are placed at the four intersection points.
    -   **Function:** Creates balanced compositions, improves visual interest, avoids central placement.

3.  ### 📊 :green[Visual Hierarchy]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** The arrangement of elements according to their importance, controlling the order in which viewers notice information.
    -   **How to Establish:** Adjusting size, color, contrast, typography, position, and spacing.

4.  ### ➡️ :green[Leading Lines]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** Lines or visual paths that naturally direct the viewer's eyes toward an important subject.
    -   :yellow[**Examples:**] Roads, railways, buildings, shadows, repeated objects.

5.  ### ⚪ :green[Negative Space]
    -   **:green[Definition:]** The empty area surrounding or between the main subjects in a composition; it is an intentional design element.
    -   **Function:** Highlights the main subject, improves readability, reduces clutter, creates balance, makes designs cleaner.
